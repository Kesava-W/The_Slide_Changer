# Chapter 15 — The Warning

Kartik arrived the next afternoon in a car nicer than anything Dhriti had expected, dressed in the careful way men dressed when they'd been told, explicitly, that appearances mattered for this particular Sunday - a well-cut shirt, a watch he checked twice in the first ten minutes for no reason connected to the time.

Her mother had arranged it fast, faster than these things usually moved, and Dhriti understood exactly what that speed meant without either of them saying it out loud: Saroja had heard about Sahishnu the night before and gone looking for a counterweight before the week was out.

The formal introductions happened in the drawing room, her mother's good cups brought out, Prakash making careful, polite conversation about Kartik's family, his work - something in sales, mid-level, comfortable enough to check the boxes Saroja cared about without needing further explanation. Kartik was easy in the room, charming in the effortless way of someone who'd done this exact performance several times before and had long since smoothed out any awkwardness from the process. He laughed at the right moments. He complimented the tea. He asked Dhriti polite, low-stakes questions about her job that required nothing real from either of them.

"Your mother tells me you're in HR," Kartik said at one point, leaning back in the good armchair with the easy confidence of a man who'd never once worried about being appraised the way he was currently appraising the room. "Good field. Stable. My sister did an HR diploma, actually, though she's not working now - got married, decided to focus on the family instead."

"Some people decide that. I haven't." Dhriti kept it light, though she caught her mother's quick, warning glance from across the room, the particular look that meant *don't start*.

"No, of course. Different priorities for different people." Kartik smiled, easy, unbothered, the kind of man who'd clearly learned to let almost anything slide off him without it costing him anything. "It's a good match on paper, is what I mean. Both of us doing well for ourselves."

It was only once Saroja engineered a few minutes alone for them - tea on the balcony, the older two retreating inside with a transparency that fooled nobody - that Dhriti decided she wasn't going to play the rest of this the way her mother had scripted it.

"I should tell you something before this goes any further," she said, setting her cup down. "There's someone else. Someone I'm seeing, seriously, someone my mother doesn't approve of because of what he earns."

Kartik's easy expression didn't fully drop, but something behind it recalibrated, quickly, a man adjusting to information that changed the shape of the afternoon. "That's - unexpected. Does your mother know you're telling me this?"

"No. I'm telling you because I'd rather you hear it from me than find out later and feel like you were deceived. I don't think that's fair to do to someone." She watched him for a moment, curious despite herself about which way he'd land. "His name's Sahishnu. He's a developer, on a hospital AI project. He's good at what he does. He's earning less than my mother would like, for now."

"For now."

"He's doubled his salary in six months. I don't think 'for now' is doing the work my mother thinks it's doing."

Kartik was quiet a moment, turning his cup slowly, and when he spoke again, something in his voice had settled into a register she recognised immediately, one she'd been braced for without fully admitting it to herself. "I get where you're coming from, Dhriti, I do. But your mother's not wrong, either. Trajectory's a nice word, but it's not a guarantee. I've seen plenty of 'promising' men stay exactly where they started for ten years. Security's not nothing. If I were choosing, I'd choose the sure thing over the maybe, every time."

"That's not really an answer to what I said. I told you he's already doubled it."

"Once. Over six months. That's one data point, not a trend." He said it lightly, almost kindly, the tone of a man who genuinely believed he was being reasonable rather than dismissive. "Look, I'm not trying to be difficult. I'm just saying - your mother's instincts aren't crazy. A man who's proven himself financially is a safer bet than one who's asking you to wait and see. That's not an insult to him. It's just how the numbers actually work, in practice, for most people."

Something in Dhriti went very still, the specific stillness she recognised in herself from years of conversations exactly like this one, the stillness that meant she'd stopped debating and started deciding. "You're agreeing with her. Not staying out of it - actually agreeing."

"I mean - yes? I don't see why I'd pretend otherwise to make you feel better." He shrugged, easy, entirely unaware of how the shrug landed. "You asked me to be honest instead of polite. I'm being honest."

"You are." She held his gaze for a moment, something settling into place behind her own eyes that she didn't let show on her face - a decision, whole and complete, arriving faster than she'd expected it to, though she wasn't ready yet to say what it was, not to him, not to anyone. "I appreciate that, actually. It makes this easier."

"Makes what easier?"

"I think you should let this go, Kartik. Tell your family it didn't work out, tell my mother whatever version you're comfortable with - but let it go, before it goes any further than a Sunday afternoon and a cup of tea." She said it plainly, no threat in her tone, just the flat certainty of someone stating a fact rather than making a request. "I'm not going to end up choosing you. And I don't think you want to be the man who finds that out the embarrassing way, later, in front of both our families, instead of now, quietly, over tea."

Kartik blinked, then laughed - not unkindly, but with the easy, practised dismissiveness of a man who'd clearly decided, somewhere in the last thirty seconds, that this was a woman testing him rather than actually warning him. "That's quite a speech for someone who invited me here to talk about someone else."

"I didn't invite you. My mother did."

"Fair." He was still smiling, the whole exchange clearly filed away in his head as spirited rather than serious, a story he'd probably tell later as evidence of how interesting the girl had been, nothing more. "Look, I'm not going anywhere just because you've decided to be dramatic about a boyfriend your own mother doesn't approve of. Let's see how this actually plays out before either of us starts talking about embarrassment."

"Okay," Dhriti said, and something in the evenness of it should have told him more than his own easy laugh let him hear. "Let's see how it plays out."

He left an hour later on good terms with her parents, Saroja visibly pleased with how the afternoon had gone, already talking in the kitchen about a second meeting, dates, the practical machinery of a match moving forward exactly the way it was supposed to. Dhriti said nothing to correct any of it, not that evening.

She sat in her room afterward for a long while, turning the whole conversation over, and arrived, slowly, at a shape of a decision she wasn't ready to name out loud yet - not to her mother, not to Sahishnu, not even fully to herself in words she'd have been able to say to another person without flinching. It sat there in her, complete and quiet, the way her silence had always sat in her at the moments that mattered most.

Kartik had laughed. She thought about that laugh for a long time after he'd gone, turning it over the way you hold onto something you already know you're going to remember later, for reasons the other person doesn't yet understand.

He'd find out. She was certain of that much, even if she wasn't yet certain of anything else.

She thought, too, about the particular shape of her mother's satisfaction in the kitchen afterward - the ease in Saroja's voice, already three steps ahead, already picturing a second meeting, a date fixed, a story she could tell relatives that ended the way she'd always wanted it to end. Dhriti didn't have the heart to correct it that night, and she recognised, lying in bed later, that the not-correcting wasn't kindness. It was strategy, whether or not she was ready to call it that yet. Let her mother have her week of certainty. It would cost nothing to let it run its course a little longer, and by the time it mattered, Dhriti suspected, none of tonight's careful optimism would still be standing anyway.
