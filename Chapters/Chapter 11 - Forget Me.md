# Chapter 11 — Forget Me

She crossed the distance herself, in the end. He hadn't given her permission, not in words, but something in his silence must have read as an opening, because she closed the twenty feet between them and stopped an arm's length away, close enough that he could see her eyes were already wet, though she hadn't let anything fall yet.

"Say something," she said.

"What is there to say." Flat. Not cruel - he didn't have cruelty in him for this, whatever else he had. "You lied. I found out. That's the whole story, isn't it."

"That's not the whole story and you know it."

"Isn't it." He looked at her properly now, for the first time since she'd walked in, and made himself hold the look even though every instinct in him wanted to find somewhere else to put his eyes. "Nine lakh a year, Dhriti. I make three. You knew that the entire time, and you built three months out of me not knowing it."

"I built three months out of wanting to actually know you before a number got in the way. That's different from lying to hurt you."

"The effect's the same either way."

She flinched at that, small, and he watched himself land the hit and felt nothing like satisfaction, only a dull, exhausted ache that this was apparently what they were now - people who landed hits on each other in a park at eight in the evening.

"I'm not going to pretend the lie doesn't matter," she said, quieter now. "It does. I know it does. But don't stand there and tell me three months meant nothing because of a number I was scared to say out loud."

"I'm not saying it meant nothing." He looked away, finally, out at the darkening path, the couple with the dog long since gone, the jogger's footsteps faded out entirely. "I'm saying it doesn't matter what it meant. Forget me, Dhriti. Move on. Find someone your family won't have to explain."

"Sahishnu-"

"I mean it."

"You don't." Her voice cracked, the tears finally slipping loose, though she didn't wipe them, didn't look away from him either, and something in the way she let herself cry without hiding it struck him as its own kind of stubbornness, refusing even her own grief the dignity of being managed. "You don't mean it, because if you meant it you wouldn't look like this saying it. You're not doing this because you've stopped wanting me. You're doing it for some other reason, and I want you to actually say it instead of hiding behind 'forget me' like that's an argument."

He didn't answer right away. The silence stretched long enough that she didn't fill it either, just stood there, waiting, arms wrapped around herself now against the evening's cooling air, and he understood that she wasn't going to let him off with the easy version of this.

"Fine," he said finally. "You want the reason. Here's the reason."

He sat back down on the bench, elbows on his knees, and didn't look at her while he said the rest of it, because looking at her while he said it felt like more than he could manage.

"Right now, this feels fine. Better than fine. You don't care about the number - I believe that, Dhriti, I do. But 'right now' isn't the part I'm afraid of." He turned the words over slowly, like he'd rehearsed them somewhere without meaning to, alone, on some bus ride he couldn't now place. "Two years from now. Three. You're at some family function, and someone asks what your husband does, and you say data entry, and you watch their face do the thing faces do. You go home to your parents for a festival and your mother's friends ask, gently, whether you're doing alright for money, whether you need anything, in that particular voice people use when they already think they know the answer. It doesn't happen once. It happens over and over, in small rooms, at weddings, at every family gathering for the rest of your life, and every single time, some part of you will have to decide whether to defend me or just let it go because defending me is exhausting and you're tired."

"That's not going to happen."

"You don't know that."

"Neither do you." She said it fast, sharp, though he pushed past it before she could build on it, needing to finish now that he'd started.

"I know what it's like to be underestimated for years at a stretch, Dhriti. I've lived inside it. Two drop years. A CGPA everyone in my family still brings up like it's a wound that never closed. A project that fell apart through no fault of mine and still somehow reads, to anyone hearing the short version, like proof I couldn't hack it. I know exactly what that does to a person, being seen as less before they've even opened their mouth - and I am not going to be the reason it happens to you too. I won't do it. I'd rather you resent me for walking away now than watch you slowly get worn down by something I could have spared you, if I'd had the sense to end it before it went any further."

"That's not your decision to make for me."

"It's the only decision I can make that actually protects you." He said it with a certainty that came from somewhere old and well-worn, a conviction he'd built slowly over years out of every small underestimation he'd ever absorbed without complaint, and it held now the way it had always held - solid, reasoned, a wall he'd built brick by brick out of his own lived experience and trusted completely, because it had never once, in all the years he'd carried it, been tested by someone standing in front of him asking him to prove it. "Society isn't kind to women who marry down, Dhriti. It just isn't."

"You keep saying that like it's a law of physics."

"I've watched it happen. Not in theory - actually watched it." He thought of Ramana's elder sister, a story Ramana had told him once, half as a joke, over the bad coffee at the data entry office - a bank manager's daughter who'd married a man three rungs below her on paper, good at his work but not paid for it yet, and how the visits home had thinned out year by year, how she'd stopped correcting the aunts who asked, every single time, whether she regretted it. Ramana told it like an anecdote, something that had happened to someone else's family. Sahishnu had never been able to hear it as anything less than a warning. "I'm not going to be the reason it happens to you."

She was quiet for a long moment, watching him, tears still tracked down her face but her voice, when it came, steadier than he'd expected.

"You've decided all of this," she said. "About my life. About what I can and can't survive. Without asking me a single thing about what I actually want."

"I'm asking you to survive something you haven't had to survive yet. That's not the same as not asking."

"Isn't it, though."

He didn't have an answer ready for that one - not because she'd broken the logic, not yet, only because the question had come at him from an angle he hadn't built a wall against. He sat with it, jaw tight, certain, still, that he was right, that the wall would hold the way it always had.

"Forget me," he said again, quieter now, because he'd run out of new ways to say it and this was the only sentence left standing. "Please. Just - move on. It's kinder, in the end. For both of us."

"You'd rather decide this alone," she said finally, "than risk being wrong about it together."

"I'd rather be alone and right than together and watch you pay for me being wrong."

"That's not a choice you get to make unilaterally, Sahishnu. Not about my life."

"It's the one thing I can actually control." His voice had gone rougher than he meant it to, the certainty starting to sound, even to his own ears, slightly more effortful than it had a few minutes ago, though he wasn't ready yet to examine why. "Everything else - my salary, the project, what people think of a CGPA on a piece of paper - none of that was ever mine to control. This is. I can control whether I let this go further and cost you something later. So I'm controlling it."

She didn't answer that. She just looked at him, something working behind her eyes that he couldn't read, and he braced himself - certain, still, entirely certain - for whatever she was about to say next.
