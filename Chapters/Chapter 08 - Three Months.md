# Chapter 8 — Three Months

The headphones were the first thing she almost got wrong.

She'd seen them in a mall display near her office, good ones, the kind that cost more than most people spent on a month of small pleasures put together, and had bought them without doing the arithmetic she usually did automatically now, the constant background calculation of what a receptionist on four lakh a year could plausibly afford to give someone. She caught the mistake in the cab on the way to meet him, turned the box over in her hands, and by the time she handed it to him had already rehearsed the casual shrug that came with it.

"They were on sale," she said. "Big discount, some festival thing. I wouldn't have bought them otherwise."

Sahishnu turned the box over, clearly clocking the brand, clearly doing a little arithmetic of his own. "That's a generous sale."

"It's Hyderabad. Everything's on sale if you catch it at the right minute." She took the box back from him before he could look too closely at the price sticker she hadn't quite managed to peel off in the cab, and pressed it into his hands again, changing the subject with the practised ease of someone who'd already had to do this once or twice before. "Just take it. You need better headphones than whatever ancient thing you're using for the bus."

He let it go - not fully convinced, she could tell, something faintly puzzled sitting behind his eyes for a second longer than the moment needed - but he let it go, the way people let things go when the alternative is making a fuss over a gift someone clearly wanted to give. He wore them on their next date, self-consciously pleased, and didn't bring up the price again.

---

The cinema outings became their easiest rhythm - Friday evenings, the nine o'clock show at the multiplex near his flat because it was cheaper than the one near hers and neither of them had said so out loud, both simply gravitating there without discussing it. He always insisted on buying the tickets. She always found some way to cover the popcorn and the drinks before he could reach for his wallet, a small, quiet negotiation neither of them ever named directly - him keeping the larger, visible cost; her absorbing the smaller ones before he noticed there was a competition happening at all.

One evening, mid-film, in the dark, he leaned over during a lull in whatever forgettable action sequence was playing and murmured, "You always order too much popcorn."

"There's no such thing as too much popcorn."

"There's a whole bucket left over every single time."

"That's called planning ahead. For later."

"You don't eat popcorn 'later,' Dhriti, it goes stale in forty minutes."

"Speak for yourself. I have a system." She didn't, and they both knew she didn't, and he laughed - quietly, so as not to disturb the row in front of them, one hand briefly covering his mouth - in a way that made the couple two seats down glance over, mildly annoyed, and made Dhriti feel something close to unreasonably pleased with herself for having caused it.

---

The restaurant dates were where the gap came closest to the surface, though neither of them ever quite let it break through. He picked modest places, always - not embarrassed by them, exactly, more matter-of-fact about what made sense on his budget, and she'd learned by the second month never to suggest anywhere that would make the choice feel like a test he hadn't been told he was taking. She found other ways to spend on him instead, sideways ways - a good bottle of something for no occasion at all, a birthday two months early because "I just saw it and thought of you," small excesses dressed up as spontaneity so they never had to be weighed against his own numbers directly.

He noticed more of it than she gave him credit for, she suspected, though he never said so outright - just a certain quietness some evenings, a beat of hesitation before accepting something, gone as quickly as it arrived. She told herself it didn't matter yet. Later, when he knew the truth of her, the small excesses would make sense retroactively, folded into a fuller picture instead of standing out as anomalies. She believed that more than she examined it.

There was one evening, at a small Andhra place he liked near his flat, when it nearly surfaced properly. The bill came, he reached for it the way he always did, and she let him have it, the way she always let him have the visible ones - but he paused over the total a beat longer than usual, doing some silent sum in his head, and she watched something cross his face that she recognised immediately as a man recalculating what he could afford to order next time.

"We could go somewhere cheaper," he said, not quite meeting her eyes, setting a few notes down on the little tray. "I don't want you thinking this is the ceiling of what I can manage, but also - I'd rather just say it than have you notice and not say anything."

"I like this place," she said, which was true, and easier than the rest of what she wanted to say. "I'm not measuring you against a ceiling, Sahi."

"Everyone measures against something."

"Fine. Measure me against whether I complain about the food. I haven't, have I?"

That got a small, reluctant smile out of him, the tension in his shoulders easing back down by a degree. "No. You haven't." He didn't bring it up again that night, but she noticed he ordered one dish less expensive than he might have otherwise, a small, private adjustment she pretended not to clock, and made up for quietly two days later by insisting - over his objection - on paying for the auto both ways on their next outing, a cost small enough that he couldn't build a whole argument out of refusing it.

---

By the third month, it had stopped feeling like something either of them was managing and started feeling, simply, like their life - Friday cinema, the occasional weeknight call that ran two hours longer than either of them intended, her parents beginning to ask, lightly, who she kept disappearing to see, questions she deflected without quite lying, because "someone I met, it's still early" wasn't false, exactly, it just left out the part where it hadn't felt early in weeks.

She caught herself, one evening, describing something that had happened at work and instinctively reaching for her phone to tell him about it before she'd consciously decided to - the unthinking reflex of someone who'd stopped treating him as a person she was dating and started treating him as a person she simply told things to, first, before anyone else. She noticed the reflex and sat with it a moment, a little unsettled by how naturally it had arrived.

Three months in, and it had stopped feeling like something that could still end easily. That, more than anything else, was what sat with her on the drive home that night - not the headphones, not the popcorn, not even the quiet hesitation she sometimes caught in him over a restaurant bill. Just the plain, unmanageable fact that this had become something with real weight to it now, and weight, once it accumulated, didn't come off again without a cost.

Her mother asked about him again the next morning, over breakfast, in the same light, testing tone she always used for questions she actually cared about the answers to. "This one you keep disappearing to see. Is it serious?"

"It's early," Dhriti said, which by now was less an answer than a habit, a sentence worn smooth from repeated use.

"Early doesn't usually come with headphones as gifts," her mother said, not looking up from the newspaper, and Dhriti realised, not for the first time, that her mother noticed considerably more than she let on, and simply chose, for now, which threads to pull.

She didn't let herself finish the thought all the way to where it was heading. There would be time for that later. For now, she let herself just be happy, three months in, the gap still unfound, the ground beneath them still, for a little while longer, solid.
