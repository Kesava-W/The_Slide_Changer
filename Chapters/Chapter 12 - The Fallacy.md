# Chapter 12 — The Fallacy

Dhriti stood there a moment longer, watching him watch her, and felt the shape of the evening narrow down to something very small and very precise - not the lie anymore, not Manish, not even the three months behind them. Just the one sentence he'd built his whole argument on, sitting there between them, waiting to be tested properly for the first time.

She let his silence sit a moment longer before she asked the question she'd actually come here to ask - not the one about the lie, that one she'd already half-answered by standing here at all. This one was smaller, and she asked it the way she asked most things that mattered: plainly, no cushioning around it.

"Are you sure your salary won't increase? Ever?"

Sahishnu blinked, thrown by the turn of it. "What?"

"You said it like a certainty. That in two, three years, I'd be sitting at some family function explaining a husband who earns what you earn now. You said it like it was already decided." She held his eyes, steady, though her own were still wet from before. "So I'm asking you plainly. Are you certain your salary will never move? That you'll be at three lakh a year forever?"

"I don't - " He stopped, the sentence catching somewhere he clearly hadn't expected it to catch. "I don't know that. Nobody knows that."

"You don't know that."

"No."

"But you were certain enough about it two minutes ago to end this over it." She kept her voice level, not triumphant, not cruel - just laying the two sentences next to each other so he could see what she was seeing. "You told me, with total confidence, what my life would look like in three years. And now, when I ask you to actually stand behind that certainty, you're telling me you don't know."

He opened his mouth, and nothing came out that sounded like an argument, only the beginning of one that dissolved before it found its shape. She watched him reach for the wall he'd built five minutes ago - solid, reasoned, load-bearing - and find, for the first time, that a hand had gone through it somewhere and he couldn't immediately locate where.

"That's not - " he started again. "That's not the same thing. I know the odds. I know what happens to people like me."

"You know what happened to one person you heard about from Ramana. That's not odds, Sahishnu. That's a story you've decided to believe because believing it lets you make this decision without having to actually risk anything." She took a step closer, close enough now that she could see the muscle working in his jaw, the effort it was costing him to keep standing there and hold the argument together. "You're not protecting me from a certainty. You're protecting yourself from a possibility. There's a difference, and I think some part of you already knows it, or you wouldn't be struggling this hard to answer a simple question."

"It's not that simple."

"It is that simple. How are you so sure that what you told me would happen, would actually happen?"

He didn't answer. She watched him try, watched his mouth open around the start of something and find nothing solid to stand on, and understood, watching it happen, that she'd just asked him the one question his whole argument had never had to survive before - not from anyone, not even from himself, because he'd built it alone, in his own head, over years, and nobody had ever stood in front of him and made him defend it out loud.

"I know about the project," she said, quieter now, letting the fight go out of her own voice to make room for what came next. "The hospital. The funding that got pulled. Manish didn't tell me the details - I found out on my own, before I ever asked him to arrange anything. I know it wasn't your fault. I know you built something real for two years and it fell apart because a hospital ran out of money, not because you weren't good enough."

"Dhriti-"

"Let me finish." She held his gaze, unflinching, though something in her own chest had started to ache watching him standing there, visibly bracing for whatever came next like it might be another blow. She thought of the photo again, the comment underneath it she'd never told him about - *this one deserved so much more credit than he ever took for himself* - a stranger's sentence that had done more to convince her than anything she'd have been able to find out on her own. "I know all of that, and it changed nothing about what I felt when I saw your photo the first time. It hasn't changed anything since. I don't believe in you despite the project failing, Sahishnu. I believe in you because of how you handled it failing - because you didn't blame anyone, you didn't fall apart, you just went and found the next thing and did it well enough that a hospital wanted you back eventually, even if that hasn't happened yet. That's not nothing. That's not a man who's going to be stuck at three lakh a year forever. That's a man who hasn't been given a fair shot yet, and I don't see why I should let a fair shot pass me by just because you've decided in advance that I shouldn't."

He turned away from her then - not sharply, not in anger, just a slow half-turn, shoulders dropping, like something in him had finally stopped holding its shape.

She watched his back for a moment, the particular stillness of him breaking down into something she hadn't seen from him before, in three months of dates and calls and a hundred small ordinary evenings - his shoulders moving, once, then again, the sound that came with it small and swallowed, a man trying, uselessly, to keep something private that had already stopped being containable.

"Sahishnu."

"Forget me," he said, and his voice had gone thick, unrecognisable, nothing left of the flat certainty from ten minutes ago. "Please. Just - go home. Forget this. Forget me."

He didn't mean it. She could hear that he didn't mean it, in the way the words came out - not an argument anymore, just a last reflex, a hand grabbing for the wall even after the wall had already come down.

She crossed the last of the distance between them and took his hand. He didn't pull it away.

"No," she said.

"Dhriti-"

"No. I'm not going anywhere, and I'm not forgetting anything, and you can stand there and cry about it as long as you need to, but I'm still going to be standing here when you're done." She said it steadily, though her own voice had gone rougher than she meant it to by the end of the sentence. "You don't get to decide this alone. You tried, and it didn't hold up to one honest question, and I think you know that now."

He turned back to face her, finally - eyes wet, unguarded in a way she understood, watching it, that she was probably the only person alive who'd ever seen him look like this, and that he hated being seen like this even as some other part of him had clearly stopped being able to stop it. He didn't say anything. He didn't have to.

She reached up and wiped at his face, once, with her thumb, an unthinking gesture that felt more natural than anything else she'd done all evening, and he closed his eyes at the touch of it, breath catching audibly.

"I'm staying," she said again, quieter this time, not an argument anymore, just a fact she was laying down between them for good. "Whatever comes in three years, whatever anyone says at whatever function - I'm staying. That's my decision. You don't get to make it for me."

He didn't answer with words. He pulled her in instead, both arms around her, his face against her hair, and she felt him shake once, fully, before he went still against her - not composed, not the flat, unreadable calm she'd watched him wear at the training session, just quiet, finally, in a way that felt earned rather than performed.

The park had gone properly dark around them by then, streetlights flickering on somewhere behind the gulmohar tree, the evening's noise thinned down to almost nothing. Neither of them moved for a long while. Neither of them needed to say anything else.

Somewhere behind them, a stray auto rattled past the far gate, headlights sweeping briefly across the path before it turned and was gone. Dhriti felt him breathe out, slow, against her shoulder, and understood without being told that whatever had just happened here - whatever this evening had cost him to arrive at - wasn't going to need repeating. He wasn't going to say *forget me* again. She didn't need him to say anything else at all, not tonight. She just stood there, holding on, and let him take whatever time he needed to come back to himself.

When he finally pulled back, enough to look at her properly, his eyes were still red, his face still unguarded in a way she suspected he'd spend the next few days quietly regretting, in private, the way he regretted most things he considered weakness in himself. She didn't let him retreat into that regret yet. She reached up and touched his face once more, lightly, and said the only thing left worth saying.

"Sahi," she said. "We're okay."

He didn't answer in words. He just nodded, once, and pulled her back in.
