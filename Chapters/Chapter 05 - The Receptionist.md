# Chapter 5 — The Receptionist

She let three days pass before she called him back, not because she'd needed the time to decide, but because she'd learned, over the years, that Manish negotiated better with himself in her absence than in her presence - that if she gave him just enough silence, he'd talk himself halfway to yes before she ever said a word.

"I've thought about it," she said, no preamble, the moment he picked up.

"Please tell me 'it' isn't what I think it is."

"Tell him I'm a receptionist."

The silence on the other end went on long enough that Dhriti checked her screen to make sure the call hadn't dropped.

"Say that again," Manish said.

"When you tell him about me - tell him I work reception. Front desk, somewhere unremarkable. And tell him my salary's close to his. Three and a half, four lakh, something in that range." She said it evenly, the way she might have read out a set of numbers for a spreadsheet, and some distant part of her registered how strange that evenness would sound to anyone listening in. "Not receptionist at my actual company, obviously. Somewhere smaller. Somewhere he'd have no reason to ever check."

"Dhriti." Manish's voice had gone flat in a way she hadn't heard from him before, flat in the way of someone who'd stopped finding something funny partway through hearing it. "You want me to lie to my family friend's face about who you are, so that he'll agree to meet you."

"I want you to remove an obstacle that has nothing to do with whether the two of us would actually suit each other. That's different."

"It is not different. It's a lie. A specific, detailed lie, that I have to deliver, to his face, and then presumably maintain for as long as this - whatever this is - goes on." He exhaled hard enough that she heard it crackle through the speaker. "And what happens when he finds out? Because he will find out, Dhriti, eventually people find out these things, and when he does, who does he blame? Not you. Me. I'm the one who told him."

"He won't find out if it's handled carefully."

"You cannot promise that. Nobody can promise that." Manish's voice climbed, not quite shouting but close to it, closer than she'd heard from him in years. "This isn't like the Sahishnu thing three days ago, where I just confirmed a name. This is asking me to actively construct a lie and hand it to someone who trusts me completely, about something he'd absolutely reject if he had the real information. You're asking me to take away his ability to choose, Dhriti. That's what this is. I'm not doing it."

Something in her chest tightened at that - not guilt, not exactly, or not only that, but something adjacent to it, a recognition she didn't have time to sit with properly before she pushed past it. She'd expected the argument. She hadn't expected it to land quite so precisely.

"He's rejecting a version of me that doesn't exist," she said. "A rich HR woman who'll look down on him. That's not who I am, Manish, you know that. I'm asking you to remove a fiction he's built in his own head, not to deceive the actual man."

"That's a very convenient way to describe lying about your salary."

"It's temporary. Once he knows me - actually knows me - none of it will matter. People don't fall for spreadsheets, Manish. They fall for people."

"And if he doesn't get the chance to know you, because he finds out on date three and walks, and by then it's not a fiction anymore, it's a betrayal he lived inside of? What then?"

Dhriti didn't have an answer for that one ready, not one she was willing to say out loud, and the pause stretched long enough that Manish must have taken it as an opening.

"I'm not doing it," he said again, quieter this time, the anger draining out and something more tired taking its place. "I like you, Dhriti. I do. But I'm not going to lie to Sahishnu's face for you. Find another way."

She sat with that for a moment, turning her phone over once in her hand, weighing something she'd been hoping she wouldn't have to weigh. There was a version of tonight where she said okay, thanked him for his honesty, and let the whole idea die quietly - the reasonable version, the version that cost her nothing and cost Manish nothing either. She weighed that version first, gave it its fair chance. It didn't survive contact with how certain she still felt, underneath the argument, that she was right about this - not right that lying was good, but right that the man on the other end of it deserved a chance he wasn't going to get any other way. She knew the difference between those two kinds of right. She chose to act on the second one anyway.

She thought of the other thing then - the thing she'd been carrying for months without any intention of ever using it, filed away the way you file away a fact about a friend that's none of your business, right up until the moment it becomes useful. She didn't like that she still had it. She used it anyway.

"Priya," she said.

The silence that followed was a different silence altogether - not thinking, not composing an argument. Just still.

"What did you say," Manish said, very carefully.

"Priya. From your college. The one you've been seeing for over a year now, the one your parents don't know exists, because her family's the wrong caste for the conversation you haven't had the courage to start." Dhriti kept her voice level, though she could feel her own pulse in her throat as she said it, the sound of a door she was choosing to walk through even while some part of her was already flinching at the sound of it closing. "I know about her. I've known for a while. I never said anything, because it wasn't mine to say anything about."

"Are you - " Manish's voice had gone thin, stripped of its earlier heat entirely, replaced by something closer to fear, and hearing it land that way made something in Dhriti's stomach drop even as she kept going. "Are you threatening to tell my parents about Priya."

"I'm telling you that I know," Dhriti said, which wasn't quite an answer, and both of them heard that it wasn't.

"Dhriti." Just her name this time, nothing after it, the way you'd say someone's name if you were watching them do something you hadn't believed them capable of five minutes earlier.

"I don't want to do this," she said, and meant it more than she'd expected to, the words coming out rougher than she'd planned them. "I've never wanted to do this. But I need this one thing, Manish, and I need it enough that I'm willing to do something I'm not proud of to get it. That's the truth. I'm not going to dress it up as anything nicer than what it is."

There was a long stretch of nothing on the line - long enough that she almost said his name to check he hadn't hung up, before he finally spoke, and when he did, his voice had gone hollow in a way that made her want to take all of it back, and she didn't, because taking it back now would have cost her the one thing she'd actually come here for.

"Fine," he said. "Fine. I'll tell him what you want me to tell him."

"Manish-"

"Don't." It came out sharp, sharper than anything he'd said yet, and she stopped. "Don't say anything else right now. Just - I'll do it. I'll go see him this week. You'll get what you want."

He hung up before she could say anything back - no goodbye, no last word, just the flat tone of a call ended from the other side, and Dhriti sat there in her room holding the phone against her ear a moment longer than she needed to, listening to nothing.

She didn't feel triumphant. She'd half-expected to, in some small ugly corner of herself she wasn't proud of admitting existed, and its absence surprised her more than the guilt did. What she felt instead was something closer to a debt accruing somewhere she couldn't yet see the size of - a thing she'd done to a friend who hadn't deserved it, in service of a man who didn't yet know she existed, and she made herself sit with that discomfort rather than reaching immediately for the comfort of telling herself it had been necessary.

It had been necessary. She still believed that, turning it over one more time in the quiet of her room. That didn't make it clean.

She thought, briefly, about what Manish would do next - whether he'd sit with this alone tonight, or call someone, or just go quiet the way people did when a piece of ground they'd trusted turned out not to be solid. She didn't know him well enough, outside of exactly these kinds of favours, to guess which. That was its own small, uncomfortable admission - that she'd spent years treating him as a resource she could call on, and only now, having just proven the worst version of that instinct true, did it occur to her to wonder what his own week looked like when she wasn't in it.

She put the phone down, finally, and didn't call anyone else that night.
