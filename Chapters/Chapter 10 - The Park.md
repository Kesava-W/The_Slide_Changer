# Chapter 10 — The Park

Manish texted him at six. *Can we talk. The park near your place, 7?*

Sahishnu had been expecting the text since noon, in the vague way you expect weather you can already see building on the horizon, and he'd spent the hours between then and now doing nothing productive with the waiting - sitting in his room with the fan on, not eating the lunch he'd bought and left untouched on the table, running the morning's forty minutes over and over until the edges of it had gone smooth from handling.

Vijay had called around four, some unrelated update about a former colleague from the project, and Sahishnu had let it go to voicemail without meaning to lie about being busy, and then not called back, because there wasn't a version of the conversation he could have right now that didn't eventually arrive at the one thing he wasn't ready to say out loud to anyone. He'd showered, changed his shirt twice for no reason he could name, and left for the park twenty minutes earlier than he needed to, unable to sit in his room a moment longer with the fan pushing the same warm air around.

He got to the park first. Sat on the same bench he'd sat on most evenings for two years, the one under the gulmohar tree that dropped its orange flowers across the path every March, though it was well past that now, the tree just green and unremarkable in the evening light. He didn't watch for Manish arriving. He just waited, hands loose in his lap, in the particular stillness he'd been wearing all day like a second skin.

Manish sat down beside him without a greeting, which told Sahishnu something about how the conversation was going to go before either of them had said a word.

"You lied to me," Sahishnu said. Flat. Not a question.

"Yes."

"About her job. Her salary. All of it."

"Yes." Manish didn't look away when he said it, didn't reach for a smaller word than *lied*, and something in Sahishnu registered that - not gratitude, not yet, but the beginning of a distinction between what Manish had done and how he was choosing to answer for it now. "I told you she worked reception. That her salary was close to yours. Neither of those things is true. She's in HR - proper HR, corporate, and she earns close to nine lakh a year. I've known both those things since before I ever said a word to you."

Sahishnu didn't say anything for a moment. Hearing the actual number - nine lakh, three times what he made, more than three times - landed differently than the morning's slow, sick recalculation had. The morning had been abstract, a shape he could feel but not yet measure. This was the measurement.

"Why," he said.

Manish exhaled, long, and Sahishnu understood, watching him, that this part of the conversation had also been rehearsed, the way his own opening line had been. "She found your profile. From the hospital project. Something about a photo, a comment someone left under it - I don't fully understand what did it, but it did something to her, and she called me and asked who you were." Manish kept his eyes forward, on the empty path in front of the bench, not on Sahishnu. "I told her the truth about you - who you were, your situation. And I told her the truth about how you'd react if you knew hers. I said you'd assume the worst and refuse a first date before it ever happened, purely on the number, without ever actually meeting her. I believed that. I still believe it, honestly."

"So you decided for me. That I wasn't capable of hearing the truth and deciding for myself."

"No - I mean, yes, in a way, and I've thought about that a lot since. But it wasn't just my call. She asked me to lie, and when I said no, she found a way to make me say yes anyway." Manish's jaw tightened. "She knows about Priya. About my parents not knowing. She told me she'd tell them if I didn't help her."

That landed somewhere Sahishnu hadn't been braced for - not softening the anger exactly, but redirecting a piece of it, sharpening his sense of the shape of what had actually happened here. "She blackmailed you."

"Yes."

"And you didn't tell me. Any of this. For three months."

"What was I supposed to say, Sahishnu? Hey, by the way, the woman you've fallen for lied about who she is to get you to meet her, and I helped her do it because she threatened to out my relationship to my parents?" Manish's voice had climbed, finally, some of the rehearsed calm cracking under it. "I was scared. I am still scared. I'm not proud of any of this, and I'm not going to sit here and pretend I had some noble reason for going along with it. I didn't. I folded, because I had something to lose and she knew exactly how to make me lose it."

Sahishnu sat with the sentence a moment, silently, and found there was still more underneath it he needed to ask, even if none of it was going to change the answer he already had. "The headphones," he said. "The gifts. All of it - was any of that her actually being careless, or was I just meant to never notice?"

"I don't know the specifics of what she bought you. That part wasn't mine to manage." Manish shook his head slowly. "But knowing her, I don't think careless is the right word for anything she does. She probably knew exactly what she was risking every time and decided it was worth it anyway. That's not an excuse. I just don't think you're dealing with someone who does things by accident."

"And her feelings for me. Are those real, or was that part of the arrangement too?"

Manish turned to look at him properly for the first time since sitting down. "That part's real, Sahishnu. I've known her a long time. I've never seen her do anything like this for anyone else - not the calling, not the persistence, not the risk she took blackmailing her own friend over it. She didn't lie to get something small out of you. She lied because she wanted this, specifically, badly enough to do something she knew was wrong to get it. I'm not defending the method. I'm telling you the feeling underneath it isn't part of the lie."

Sahishnu sat with that, jaw tight, staring at the same stretch of empty path Manish had been staring at, and didn't trust himself to say anything for a long moment.

That was when Dhriti walked in.

She came from the far entrance, the one near the tea stalls, and stopped a good twenty feet short of the bench the moment she registered both of them sitting there - close enough to see she'd interrupted something, too far to have heard the specifics of what. Her face, for once, wasn't doing the composed thing he'd watched it do all morning. It looked, instead, like someone who'd walked toward a conversation she badly needed to have and found herself suddenly unsure whether she still had the right to be part of it.

Sahishnu's eyes moved from her to Manish and something in him - fast, ugly, more available than the anger he actually meant for her - found its target in the man sitting next to him instead.

"You should go," he said to Manish. Quiet. Final.

"Sahishnu-"

"You lied to me for three months and let me build something on top of it. I don't want to look at you right now. Go."

Manish held his gaze a second longer, something apologetic and something almost relieved crossing his face in quick succession - relieved, Sahishnu understood distantly, to finally be released from a role he'd never wanted in the first place - and then he stood, glanced once at Dhriti with an expression that wasn't quite forgiveness-seeking and wasn't quite anger either, and walked past her out of the park without another word.

That left the two of them. The bench. The unremarkable green tree that wasn't dropping flowers this time of year. The distance Dhriti hadn't yet closed, standing there at the edge of the path, waiting - Sahishnu understood, watching her not move - for him to decide whether she was allowed to close it herself.

Neither of them said anything for a long moment. The park went on around them, indifferent - a couple walking a dog near the far gate, an evening jogger's footsteps fading in and out of hearing, the whole ordinary machinery of a Tuesday evening continuing on either side of the two of them like nothing in it had just come apart. Dhriti hadn't moved from where she'd stopped. Sahishnu found, watching her stand there, that some part of him had expected her to come to him regardless of whether he wanted her to - the same directness Manish had just described, the woman who didn't do half-measures - and the fact that she hadn't, that she was waiting for permission she'd never once waited for in three months of knowing him, felt like its own kind of information, one he wasn't ready to weigh yet.

"Dhriti," Sahishnu said finally, and stopped there, because he didn't yet know what sentence was supposed to come after her name, only that something had to.
