# Chapter 6 — The Go-Between

Priya called him the morning after, before he'd even left for work, and asked why he sounded like someone had died.

"Nobody died," Manish said, sitting on the edge of his bed, phone wedged against his shoulder while he buttoned a shirt he wasn't paying attention to. "Long night, that's all."

"You're a terrible liar. I've told you this before." Her voice had the particular warmth in it that always made the rest of his life feel like something he was renting rather than something he actually lived in - a warmth he got maybe forty minutes of most days, on calls like this one, squeezed between the version of himself his parents knew and the version that existed only for her. "Tell me."

He didn't tell her. Not the whole of it - not that Dhriti had found out about her, had known for months apparently, had said her name out loud like it was nothing, like it wasn't the one thing in his life that could still unmake several other things if it landed in the wrong ears. He told Priya a smaller, sanded-down version - a favour someone was asking him for, something he didn't love doing, nothing to worry about - and hated himself a little for how practised the sanding-down had become. He'd gotten good at giving people smaller versions of things. He wasn't sure when that had started being true of him, or whether it counted as a skill or a symptom.

"You'll tell me properly eventually," Priya said, not pushing, which was somehow worse than if she had. "You always do."

"Eventually," he agreed, and meant it, and didn't know when eventually was going to be.

He hung up and sat there a moment longer, the shirt half-buttoned, thinking about the particular shape of the thing Dhriti had done to him last night. It wasn't that she'd threatened him, exactly - she hadn't said if you don't do this, I'll tell your parents, not in those words. She hadn't needed to. She'd said Priya's name, and the caste his parents would never accept without a fight neither he nor Priya had the stomach for yet, and let the rest sit there, implied, heavier for being unsaid. That was almost worse. A direct threat he could have argued with. This was just a fact she'd handed him, wrapped in nothing, and trusted him to understand what it cost.

He did understand. That was the whole problem.

His parents were two rooms away, his mother already up and moving pots around in the kitchen, the ordinary domestic noise of a morning that had no idea what he was carrying through it. He thought, not for the first time, about how close the two halves of his life sat to each other without ever touching - Priya on the phone at seven, his mother's voice calling out that breakfast was ready at seven-fifteen, both of them entirely unaware of how thin the wall between them actually was, and how easily someone else could knock it down if she decided she needed to.

He finished buttoning the shirt and went in to eat, and sat through breakfast answering his mother's questions about work in the flat, agreeable voice he'd perfected for exactly this purpose, while some other part of him ran the numbers on what Dhriti actually knew - how long she'd known, who else might know, whether "I've known for a while" meant weeks or meant since the beginning, back when he'd been careless enough, in some unguarded moment he couldn't now place, to let something slip in front of her. He couldn't remember the moment. That frightened him more than the threat itself - that he had no idea how the door had come open, only that it had, and that someone else now had a hand on it.

The pressure hadn't lifted, doing what Dhriti asked. If anything it sat heavier now, because now there were two people who could end things badly for him instead of one - Dhriti, who'd shown him exactly how far she'd go, and his own parents, still blessedly ignorant, still just two rooms away making breakfast like nothing in the house had changed.

---

He went to see Sahishnu that Saturday, at the small flat Sahishnu shared with two other operators near the hospital, and spent most of the auto ride there rehearsing a version of the conversation that felt less like a betrayal and more like a favour, the way Dhriti had described it on the phone. It didn't hold up under its own rehearsal. By the time the auto pulled up outside, he'd stopped trying to make it feel like anything other than what it was.

Sahishnu opened the door in a T-shirt with a hole starting at the collar, looking pleased in the low-key way he always looked pleased to see Manish, which made the next ten minutes considerably harder than Manish had braced for.

"Didn't expect you on a Saturday," Sahishnu said, waving him in, clearing a stack of forms off the one good chair. "Everything okay?"

"Everything's fine. I wanted to talk to you about something, actually." Manish sat, hands folded between his knees, and found himself stalling on the easy small talk longer than he needed to - how's the data entry job, has Vijay called about anything, has his father stopped asking about it yet - anything that wasn't the reason he'd actually come.

"You're doing the thing," Sahishnu said eventually, not unkindly, "where you talk about everything except the thing you came to talk about."

Manish exhaled. There wasn't a version of this that got easier by waiting. "There's someone. A friend of mine - Dhriti. She saw a photo of you somewhere, from the hospital project, and she asked me about you."

Something shifted in Sahishnu's face - not suspicion exactly, more like a door opening a crack, cautious about how far. "Asked you what."

"Who you were. Whether you'd be open to meeting someone." Manish kept his voice level, the way he'd have delivered any other piece of news, and hated how easily the practised flatness came to him now. "She's - she works reception. Some smaller firm, I don't remember the exact name. Salary's in your range, maybe a bit more, nothing dramatic."

He watched Sahishnu's face do the thing he'd been half-dreading and half-hoping for, though he hadn't let himself admit the hoping part until he saw it happen - a kind of loosening, relief moving through him visibly, the wariness that usually sat behind Sahishnu's eyes when anyone brought up meeting someone new simply not showing up this time. Comparable salary. No gap to feel small across. Nothing here that was going to make him brace for the moment she found out what he actually earned and quietly recalculated her interest.

"What's she like," Sahishnu asked, and there was an eagerness in it Manish hadn't heard from him in the two years since the project fell through - not performed, not polite interest, just genuinely, unguardedly curious in a way that made the lie sitting between them feel enormous and Manish feel very small for having built it.

"She's - " Manish stopped, searching for something true enough to say that didn't touch the part that wasn't. "She's sharp. Funny, in a dry way. Decisive - once she wants something, she doesn't really do half-measures." All true. All safely true, none of it the salary, none of it the thing he was actually here to arrange.

"And she wants to meet me."

"She does."

Sahishnu sat back, something almost boyish crossing his face for a second before he visibly reined it in, catching himself being too obviously pleased and trying, unsuccessfully, to look more measured about it. "Okay," he said. "Yeah. I mean - yeah, set it up. Why not."

It had been that easy. Manish sat there a moment, absorbing how easy it had been, and felt the guilt he'd been braced for arrive a full beat too late - Sahishnu was already talking logistics, which evening worked, whether Manish thought a coffee place or something more than that for a first meeting, entirely unaware that the ground under this conversation wasn't ground at all.

"I'll set it up," Manish said, and meant it, and hated that he meant it.

He left twenty minutes later with a time and a place half-agreed, Sahishnu practically walking him to the door with an energy Manish hadn't seen out of him in longer than he could remember, and stood on the street outside the flat for a moment before calling for another auto, replaying the whole exchange in his head.

He wasn't proud of it. He wanted that on record, even if only to himself, even if nobody else was ever going to ask him to account for it - he'd done this with his eyes open, for reasons that weren't really his own, to protect something that had nothing to do with either Dhriti or Sahishnu, and he'd done it well, which somehow made it worse rather than better. A bad lie he'd have forgiven himself faster. This one had worked perfectly.

He didn't think, standing there, that it was over. Lies like this one didn't stay small. He just didn't yet know which direction it was going to grow in, or how long he had before it did.
