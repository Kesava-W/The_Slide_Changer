# Chapter 3 — Nine LPA

It was a Tuesday, and Dhriti was not looking for anything.

She'd opened the app the way she opened it most evenings after work - half out of habit, half out of the low-grade boredom of a nine-to-six that had stopped asking anything new of her months ago. It was the professional one, the one people used for job updates and congratulatory comments they'd never say out loud in person, and Dhriti kept it open more out of inertia than genuine use; she hadn't posted anything on it herself in over a year. A colleague from the Bangalore office had tagged her in a post, some panel discussion neither of them had attended, and Dhriti had scrolled past the congratulations comments the way she scrolled past most things, thumb moving faster than her attention, the evening's other half-finished tasks - a reply to her manager, a grocery list her mother had asked her to check - sitting unattended in other tabs.

Then a photo, three rows down, stopped her thumb before her brain had caught up to why.

Group shot. A hospital conference room, whiteboard half-visible behind six or seven people in a loose semicircle, the kind of photo someone takes at the end of a project milestone because someone always insists on documenting it. She didn't recognise most of the faces. She recognised, immediately and for no reason she could yet name, the one standing slightly apart from the centre of the group - not awkward, exactly, just naturally positioned at the edge of things, like someone who'd end up there in every group photo he was ever in.

She clicked through. Sahishnu, the caption said, tagging him alongside a doctor's name she didn't know - *proud of what this small team built together, even if the story isn't over yet.* Two years old, the post. She read the caption twice before she let herself look at the photo again.

It wasn't the face, or not only the face - though there was something in it, an unbothered stillness even at the edge of a group of people celebrating, that she found herself looking at longer than the moment required. It was the caption underneath, half-buried in someone else's comment thread, from a woman tagged as part of the same team: *this one deserved so much more credit than he ever took for himself.*

Dhriti read that line three times.

She didn't message him. She wasn't reckless, whatever anyone eventually decided to say about her. She closed the app, made herself a cup of tea, sat with it at the kitchen counter while her mother watched a serial in the next room, and thought about the sentence for longer than she was willing to admit to anyone.

By the time the tea had gone cold, she'd called Manish.

"It's nine o'clock," Manish said, by way of hello.

"I need you to find out something for me."

"That's not a greeting."

"Manish."

He sighed - she could hear him settle into whatever chair he was in, the particular scrape of someone who already suspected this call was going to take longer than he wanted. "Fine. What."

"There's someone I saw on - it doesn't matter where. His name's Sahishnu. Worked on some AI healthcare project a couple of years back, with a hospital, looked like it wrapped up or fell apart, I'm not sure which. Do you know him?"

There was a pause on the other end. Not a long one - two seconds, maybe three - but long enough that Dhriti, who noticed pauses the way some people noticed raised voices, caught it immediately.

"Manish."

"Why are you asking."

"That's not an answer."

Another pause, shorter this time, the sound of someone deciding there was no version of this where lying held up. "Yeah. I know him. He's - we're family friends, basically grew up around each other, our parents still meet sometimes." A beat. "Why are you asking about Sahishnu, Dhriti."

"I saw a photo of him. I liked what I saw." She said it the way she said most things she'd already decided about - plainly, no performance of hesitation to make it more palatable. "I want you to help me meet him."

"You want me to - " Manish made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "You've seen one photo."

"And a comment under it. From someone who actually worked with him." She wasn't going to explain the specific sentence, not even to Manish; it felt like something she wanted to keep, unshared, a little longer. "I'm not saying marry him tomorrow. I'm saying arrange something. A coffee. Anything. You clearly already know him well enough to do that."

"This is insane, even for you."

"Is it insane, or is it just fast?"

"It's - " Manish stopped, apparently unable to immediately locate the difference either, which Dhriti noted with some satisfaction. "Okay. Even if I said yes - and I haven't said yes - there's a problem."

"What problem."

"He's not going to want this."

Something in Dhriti's chest tightened, small and quick, the specific irritation of being told no before she'd even finished asking the question. "Why not."

Manish exhaled, the sound of someone choosing his next words carefully because he already knew they'd land badly. "Sahishnu's - things haven't gone well for him the last couple of years. That project you saw the photo from, it fell through. Funding got pulled. He's doing data entry now, Dhriti. Some back-office job, I don't even know the exact number, but it's nothing close to what a guy like him should be earning, and he knows it, and it's made him - careful. About people. Especially about anyone who might look at him and see the job title before they see him."

"So?"

"So you're at what, nine lakh a year? In HR, at a company big enough that it's basically a status symbol at this point?" Manish said it without cruelty, just as fact, the kind of fact he clearly expected to end the conversation on its own. "If he finds out that upfront, he doesn't say no because he's not interested. He says no because he's already decided what you'd think of him before you've said a word. I've watched him do this. It's not a maybe."

Dhriti sat with that for a moment, turning her now-cold tea in slow circles on the counter, the ceramic scraping faintly against the granite.

"That's it?" she said finally. "That's the whole objection? Not that he's married, not that he's seeing someone, not that he's a bad person - just that he'd assume something about me before he even sat down across from me?"

"I mean - yeah. Essentially."

"Why would he reject a first date over something he hasn't even confirmed yet? He doesn't know anything about me except a job title he's imagining. That's not caution, Manish, that's just - deciding the ending before the story's started." She wasn't asking Manish, not really. She was asking the air, working something out loud that had already half-formed in her head before she'd dialled his number. "If he's the kind of person who does that, that's one thing to find out. But somebody should at least give him the chance to be wrong about it first."

"Dhriti-"

"I'm not saying I have an answer tonight." She was, in fact, already circling one, though she wasn't ready to hand it to Manish yet - not until she'd turned it over a few more times on her own, tested how much of it she actually meant versus how much was just refusing to lose an argument. "I'm saying the objection you just gave me isn't a wall. It's a problem. Problems have solutions."

"That sounds like the beginning of something I'm going to regret agreeing to."

"You haven't agreed to anything yet." Dhriti allowed herself something close to a smile, alone in her kitchen, her mother's serial still murmuring faintly through the wall. "Just don't tell him anything yet. Give me a few days."

Manish made a noise that was half groan, half surrender - the sound, Dhriti thought, of a man who already knew how this particular story tended to go once she'd decided the ending she wanted. "A few days," he repeated, like he was trying to make it sound like a boundary rather than the beginning of losing one.

"A few days," Dhriti agreed, and hung up before he could turn it into a longer negotiation.

She sat at the counter a while longer after that, the tea gone properly cold now, replaying Manish's sentence over and over - *he'd already decided what you'd think of him before you've said a word* - until it stopped sounding like an obstacle and started sounding, quietly, like something closer to a dare.

She went back to her room and pulled the photo up again before bed, not for the face this time but for the sentence underneath it - *this one deserved so much more credit than he ever took for himself* - reading it slowly enough that she could have recited it by morning. She didn't reply to it, didn't like it, didn't do anything that would leave a trace. She just looked, the way you look at a decision you've already made and are only now getting around to admitting to yourself.

At work the next day, between two back-to-back interviews for a role she barely registered filling, she found her attention drifting sideways more than once - not to the photo itself, she'd stopped needing to look at it again, but to the shape of the problem Manish had handed her the night before. A man who decided endings before stories started. She worked at it methodically, the way she worked at most things that annoyed her, until the annoyance resolved into something closer to a plan. By the time she left the office that evening, she still hadn't called Manish back. She wanted him to feel the few days pass first.
