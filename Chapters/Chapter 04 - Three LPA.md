# Chapter 4 — Three LPA

The email came on a Thursday, forwarded to Vijay from someone in hospital administration whose name neither of them recognised, and Vijay read it out loud in the cramped side office in the flat, careful voice of someone trying to make bad news sound like a formality rather than an ending.

"Due to budgetary constraints arising from this quarter's operating losses, the hospital is suspending funding for the AI Patient Records Initiative, effective immediately." Vijay stopped there, though the email clearly went on longer than that. "There's more. It's mostly - it's the kind of more that doesn't change anything."

Sahishnu didn't say anything for a moment. He was looking at the model on his screen, the one that had finally, two weeks earlier, started producing results he was almost proud of - not perfect, but close enough that he'd allowed himself, once or twice, late at night, to imagine what it might look like finished. He closed the laptop lid slowly, the way you'd close a book you weren't planning to reopen.

"How bad are the losses," he asked.

"Bad enough that they're cutting three departments, not just us. This isn't - listen, this isn't about us. The project didn't fail. The hospital ran out of money to keep funding things that hadn't started making money back yet. That's different."

"Is it, though." Sahishnu said it quietly, not really arguing, more testing the distinction to see if it held any weight once he actually looked at it. It didn't, not for him. A project that ran out of runway before it proved itself was, by any measure that mattered outside a courtroom, a project that hadn't worked. He'd built the thing that hadn't worked. The reasons behind that felt, in the moment, like the kind of detail people offered you out of kindness rather than accuracy.

Vijay must have seen something in his face, because he came and sat across from him, elbows on the desk between them, and didn't immediately fill the silence with reassurance the way Sahishnu half-expected him to.

"I'm not going to tell you it doesn't sting," Vijay said instead. "It stings. Two years. I know what it cost you to say yes to this in the first place, and I watched you build something real out of it. That doesn't just evaporate because a finance committee ran a bad quarter."

"Doesn't it, though. Practically." Sahishnu turned the closed laptop a quarter turn on the desk, not really needing to. "There's no product. There's no job. There's a folder of code on a hospital server that's going to sit there until someone deletes it to free up space."

"There's a chance they come back to it. Once the hospital's finances settle."

"Maybe." Sahishnu didn't say it to argue the point, only because he genuinely didn't know, and had long ago stopped pretending to be certain about things he wasn't. "When does that happen, though. A year? Two?"

Vijay didn't have an answer for that, and to his credit, he didn't invent one. He just nodded, accepting the shape of the question as much as its unanswerable half, and the two of them sat there a while in the kind of silence that wasn't exactly companionable but wasn't hostile either - two men absorbing the same loss from two different angles, neither of them raising his voice about it, because raising your voice had never fixed anything either of them had lost before.

Vijay went back to his residency the following week, fully, no more stolen evenings in the side office. He didn't announce it as a defeat. He talked about it, when he talked about it at all, the way he'd talked about the funding email - a fact to be absorbed, not a wound to be nursed. "I'll keep an ear out," he told Sahishnu, the last time they sat in that office together, boxing up the two laptops that had been theirs to use and would now go back to hospital IT. "If the hospital's numbers turn around, I'm not letting this die quietly. You know that."

Sahishnu believed him. That wasn't the part he doubted.

---

His father called two days after that, not to ask how he was doing, but to ask what he was doing next - the kind of question that, in his father's voice, was never really a question at all.

"The project's over, then." Not unkind, exactly. Just final, the tone of a man who'd never fully understood what his son had been building in the first place and had waited two years, patiently enough, for it to either become something he could explain to relatives or stop being a reason his son didn't have a proper salary.

"For now," Sahishnu said.

"For now isn't a job, Sahishnu. You're twenty-five. Your cousin Ravi's already saving for a flat." A pause, the particular pause of a sentence his father had clearly rehearsed more than once before making the call. "I'm not saying give up on this - computer thing. I'm saying you can't sit at home doing it for free while you wait for a hospital to change its mind. Get something. Anything, for now. You can always go back to the other thing later, if it comes back."

Sahishnu didn't argue. There wasn't an argument in him that felt worth making - his father wasn't wrong, exactly, even if the wrongness he was avoiding wasn't the one his father was worried about. "Okay, Nanna," he said. "I'll find something."

He didn't tell his father that "anything, for now" was going to mean something considerably smaller than either of them was picturing.

---

The data entry role came through a consultancy two streets from his old college, the kind of listing that didn't ask for a portfolio or a GitHub link, only a degree certificate and the ability to type accurately for eight hours without complaint. Close to three lakh a year - a number so far beneath what two years of real, working software should have been worth that he almost laughed reading the offer letter, except laughing at it would have meant admitting how much it mattered, and he wasn't in the habit of admitting that kind of thing out loud.

He knew exactly why the number was what it was, and it wasn't complicated enough to need explaining to anyone. Six-five CGPA didn't open doors at companies that paid properly for what he could actually do; those companies filtered resumes before a human ever read them, and his never made it past the filter. The two years on the AI project should have counted for something, and on paper, to the right interviewer, maybe it would have - but the postings that might have cared about that kind of experience wanted a degree from a shorter list of colleges than his, or a portfolio link to a product that still existed, neither of which he had. What he had was a certificate with a number on it that told a story about him that wasn't true, and a two-year gap that looked, from the outside, exactly like a resume with nothing on it. The world didn't have time to ask him to explain the difference. It just moved on to the next resume.

He took the data entry job anyway. Three lakh a year, eight hours a day of typing patient intake forms and insurance codes into a system he could have redesigned in a weekend if anyone had asked him to. He didn't complain about it, not to his father, not to Vijay, not really even to himself in the privacy of his own head, because complaining had always felt, to him, like the first step toward blaming something outside himself for a shortfall that, as far as he was concerned, was still his to own.

On his first morning at the desk, badge freshly laminated, he opened the intake software - clunky, unoptimised, the kind of interface he could see three obvious improvements to within the first ten minutes - and started typing. The office was a long, low room of identical desks, twenty-odd operators typing into twenty-odd versions of the same software, the sound of it less like an office and more like rain on a tin roof, steady and indifferent. Nobody introduced themselves at length. A man two desks over, Ramana, showed him where the good filter coffee was kept without being asked, and that turned out to be the whole of his onboarding.

The work itself asked nothing of him that the AI project hadn't asked ten times over, and gave back nothing in return - no problem worth staying late for, no version of the fifth attempt that mattered more than the first. He was good at it within a week, faster than most of the room within a month, and neither fact changed the number on his payslip or the shape of his days. He typed insurance codes and patient names from nine to six, took the bus home past the same flyover twice a day, and didn't mention, to anyone who asked how the new job was, that he sometimes caught himself mentally rewriting the intake form's validation logic before he remembered it wasn't his to rewrite.

Three lakh a year. He said the number to himself once, quietly, the way you'd note a low tide - not a verdict, just a level the water happened to be at right now, and nothing said the water had to stay there.
