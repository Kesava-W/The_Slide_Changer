# Chapter 13 — Doubled

Two weeks after the park, Vijay showed up at Sahishnu's flat unannounced on a Sunday morning, still in his hospital scrubs, looking like a man who'd come straight from a shift without stopping to change because whatever he had to say couldn't wait the twenty minutes it would have taken.

"The hospital's back," he said, before Sahishnu had even finished opening the door.

"Back how?"

"Financially. The losses turned around faster than anyone expected - some new insurance tie-up, I don't fully understand the numbers, but the administration's solvent again, and they've asked about the project." Vijay was talking fast, the way he did when something had been sitting in him since he first heard it and hadn't found an outlet yet. "They want to restart it, Sahi. Properly this time - not a side thing squeezed in around your other job. They want to fund it as a real initiative."

Sahishnu stood in the doorway a moment, letting that settle, the particular disorientation of good news arriving without any warning after two weeks that had mostly been spent absorbing a different kind of news entirely. "You're serious."

"I wouldn't have come here in my scrubs if I weren't serious." Vijay grinned, some of his usual ease coming back into his face now that he'd actually said it out loud. "There's more, actually. They asked specifically about you."

"About me."

"They want to bring you on properly. Not project-based, not a stipend - an actual developer role, permanent, on their books. I told them you were the reason the last version of this worked at all, and apparently that carried more weight with the new head of IT than I expected it to." Vijay said it without any dressing up, the way he said most things he actually believed. "They're offering six. Six lakh a year, Sahi. Double what you're on now."

Sahishnu didn't say anything for a moment. He was doing the arithmetic without meaning to - three lakh, the number he'd said to himself quietly on his first morning at the data entry desk, a low tide he'd told himself wasn't permanent, and here it was, less than a year later, already turning.

"Six," he repeated, mostly to hear himself say it.

"Six. And it's real work, Sahi - the actual project, the thing you built. Not typing insurance codes into a system some intern could run." Vijay clapped him once on the shoulder, the gesture of someone who'd been waiting two years to be able to make good on a bet he'd never stopped believing in. "Told you the hospital wasn't done with you."

"You didn't tell me that. You told me you'd keep an ear out."

"Same thing, said with more patience." Vijay was already halfway to checking his phone, business mode returning even through the grin. "They want an answer by Wednesday. I already know what you're going to say. I just wanted to be the one who got to watch your face when I said it."

Sahishnu didn't need until Wednesday. He said yes there in the doorway, quietly, the same way he'd said yes two years earlier in a tea stall to an idea that had once sounded too large for him to carry - except this time there was no hesitation folded into the word, none of the old weighing of whether he deserved to be handed something this size. He'd already done the thing once. Doing it again didn't feel like a leap. It felt, instead, like picking back up something that had only ever been set down, not lost.

Vijay stayed for tea after that, the two of them sitting on the narrow balcony the way they used to sit in the side office two years back, and for a while neither of them said much of anything, just let the morning traffic noise fill in where words might otherwise have gone. It was Vijay who eventually broke it.

"You look different than you did two weeks ago," he said. "Lighter. I don't know what happened - you don't have to tell me - but whatever it was, it's good on you."

"A lot happened."

"Good, I hope."

"Mostly." Sahishnu turned his cup slowly on the little table between them, not quite ready yet to hand Vijay the whole of it, though he suspected he would, eventually, the way he told Vijay most things given enough time. "Ask me again in a few months. I think it'll be a better answer by then."

Vijay let it go without pushing, which was its own kind of gift, and the two of them sat there a while longer in the thin morning light, two years of history between them, and the beginning of something new sitting quietly on top of it.

He handed in his notice at the data entry job that Monday. His manager took it with the mild, practised disappointment of someone who'd seen operators come and go for years and had long since stopped being surprised by any of it, and processed the paperwork without much ceremony - a two-week notice period, a final paycheck calculation, the quiet administrative unwinding of a job that had never been more than a placeholder to begin with, even if neither of them had ever said so out loud.

Ramana walked him to the door on his last day, shook his hand with something like real feeling behind it, and said, "Don't forget the coffee spot when you're rich," which Sahishnu laughed at more than the joke strictly earned, mostly out of the sheer relief of being able to laugh about any of it at all. He meant to keep in touch. He suspected, even as he said so, that he probably wouldn't manage it well - not out of unkindness, just the ordinary drift of two lives that had only ever overlapped because of a desk assignment neither of them had chosen.

---

He told Dhriti that evening, over the phone, and heard her go quiet for a second before she spoke - not the surprised kind of quiet, he registered, but something closer to a woman hearing a fact land exactly where she'd always expected it eventually would.

"Six lakh," she said. "That's good, Sahi. That's really good."

"It's not nine."

"I never asked for nine." Something in her voice had an edge of amusement in it, gentle, not mocking. "I asked for capable. The number was always going to follow on its own schedule. I just didn't know it'd follow this fast."

He sat with that for a moment, phone against his ear, looking out at the same ordinary street outside his flat that had looked, two weeks ago, like the smallest version of Hyderabad he'd ever lived in. It didn't feel small tonight.

"Thank you," he said, "for not deciding I was a bad bet, back before any of this."

"I never thought you were a bad bet," she said. "I thought you were exactly the bet I wanted to make. There's a difference."

He didn't have an answer clever enough to match that, so he didn't try for one. He just stayed on the line a while longer, listening to her talk about her own day, some minor office drama she was rolling her eyes through - a colleague who'd tried to reschedule a mandatory session twice in one week, apparently, and the particular flavour of exasperation she reserved for people who treated her calendar as a suggestion - and let himself feel, quietly, without needing to name it out loud to her or to himself, that something in the shape of his life had just tilted, for the first time in longer than he could easily remember, toward the version of things he'd stopped letting himself expect.

Before they hung up, she said, almost as an afterthought, "I'm proud of you, Sahi. Just so you know I said it."

He held the phone a moment after the call ended, the flat quiet of his room settling back in around him, and let that sentence sit there a while before he let himself put the phone down.

Six lakh a year. Not the ceiling. Just the next number on the way past it.
