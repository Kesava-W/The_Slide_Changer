# Chapter 17 — Why Are You Happy?

Two months later.

She'd suspected for a week before she let herself book the appointment - a lateness she'd tracked without naming what she was tracking, a heaviness in the mornings she'd explained away twice before admitting the explanations had stopped holding. She hadn't told anyone. Not Sahishnu, not a friend, not even the corner of her own mind that liked to have things settled and filed before acting on them. She'd simply booked the test, quietly, the way she did most things she wasn't ready to discuss yet.

The clinic was a small one, tucked above a pharmacy in a lane off Somajiguda, chosen deliberately for being nowhere near anyone who might recognise her. Dhriti had booked the appointment under her own name without a second thought - there was no version of this where hiding her name would have changed anything that mattered - and sat in the waiting room turning her phone over in her hands, not really looking at it, watching instead the slow procession of other women called in ahead of her, most of them visibly further along, most of them not alone.

The doctor called her in a little after eleven. Late forties, brisk, kind in the efficient way of someone who'd delivered this particular piece of news enough times to have long since stopped treating it as an occasion.

The test took no time at all. The result took less.

"You're pregnant," the doctor said, scanning the readout once more to be certain, then setting it down. "Seven, maybe eight weeks along, by my estimate - we'll confirm properly with a scan, but the numbers are clear enough. Congratulations, if that's the word you're looking for."

Something in Dhriti's chest, some part of her she hadn't fully realised had been braced for a different sentence, released all at once. She felt herself smile - not politely, not performed, a real, unguarded thing that arrived before she'd consciously decided to let it - and sat there for a moment simply holding it, the plain fact of it settling into her like something she'd been waiting a long time to be handed.

The doctor watched her for a beat longer than the moment strictly required, something assessing in her expression now, the particular scrutiny of someone who'd seen this exact scene play out a hundred different ways and was trying to place which one this was.

"Dhriti." She said the name carefully, checking the file again, confirming something she'd apparently already half-suspected. "You're not married."

"No."

"And you're happy about this."

"Yes."

The doctor set the file down slowly, folding her hands over it, the brisk efficiency of a few minutes ago giving way to something more careful. "I have to ask, and I hope you'll forgive the bluntness - most women who come in here unmarried and two months along are not smiling the way you're smiling right now. Most of them are frightened. Some of them are here to talk about options, not celebrate a result. So I'll ask you plainly: why are you happy? What's different about your situation?"

Dhriti looked at her for a long moment. There was an honest answer sitting right behind her teeth - one that involved a mother who refused to hear an argument, a suitor who'd laughed at a warning, a man she loved who deserved better than a fight he could lose on someone else's terms - and she turned it over, briefly, testing whether any of it was a thing she was willing to say out loud to a stranger in a clinic above a pharmacy.

It wasn't. Not yet. Not to anyone.

"Thank you for the checkup," Dhriti said instead, standing, gathering her bag with hands that were steadier than she expected them to be. "I'll book the scan you mentioned."

"Dhriti-"

"I appreciate you asking." She meant that, actually, some small part of her registering that the doctor's question had come from genuine care rather than judgement, and it deserved better than being brushed past entirely. "I just don't have an answer I'm ready to give anyone yet. Including myself, maybe."

That wasn't quite true either, and she suspected the doctor could tell, watching her with an expression that had gone from clinical concern to something closer to plain curiosity, the look of someone filing a patient away as one of the more interesting ones this year.

Dhriti didn't give her anything further. She thanked her again at the door, booked the follow-up scan with the receptionist on her way out, and stepped back onto the street in the flat, bright heat of early afternoon, phone already in her hand though she had no one to call yet, nothing to say out loud to anyone until she'd said it, first, all the way through, to herself.

She walked two full blocks before she let herself stop and simply stand there, in the noise and heat of an ordinary Hyderabad street, holding a certainty she wasn't ready to name, even now, even to the only person who could actually hear it.

Her phone buzzed once in her bag - Sahishnu, a routine afternoon message about nothing in particular, asking if she'd eaten lunch yet. She looked at it for a long moment, thumb hovering, and didn't answer it right away. There would be a right time to tell him. This wasn't it, not yet, and some part of her was already quietly deciding when it would be, and what shape the telling would need to take when it finally came.

She was smiling the whole way home.
