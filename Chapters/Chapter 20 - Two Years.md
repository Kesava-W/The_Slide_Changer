# Chapter 20 — Two Years

Two years later.

The terrace was strung with balloons Sahishnu had insisted on inflating himself, badly, several of them lopsided in a way that made Dhriti laugh every time she looked at them, and their son - Arjun, two years old that afternoon, sturdy on his feet now in the particular unsteady, delighted way of a child who'd only recently discovered running was a thing he could simply decide to do - was currently attempting to eat a fistful of cake with both hands while ignoring the spoon entirely.

"He gets that from you," Sahishnu said, watching from the doorway, arms crossed, the plain fond exasperation of a father who'd clearly already lost this particular argument several times before.

"He gets that from himself. I have never once eaten cake with my hands."

"You told me once you had a 'system' for leftover popcorn. There is no system, Dhriti. There has never been a system."

"That's completely different." Dhriti didn't bother arguing further, mostly because he was right and she had no intention of admitting it.

Saroja came out onto the terrace a few minutes later, carrying a fresh plate of snacks nobody had asked for, fussing over whether the balloons were secure enough in the afternoon breeze, and knelt to wipe a smear of icing off Arjun's cheek with the particular unhurried tenderness of a grandmother who'd long since stopped keeping score of anything that had happened two years ago. She didn't say much to Dhriti directly - they'd found, in the two years since that dining-room evening, an ease that didn't require constant narration, a peace built less out of any single reconciling conversation than out of a hundred small ordinary Sundays that had slowly worn the old argument down to something neither of them needed to revisit anymore. Prakash sat nearby, unhurried, letting Arjun climb over his knees without complaint, occasionally catching Dhriti's eye across the terrace with something that had settled, over two years, into simple contentment.

At one point Dhriti caught her mother on the phone near the kitchen doorway, voice pitched for a call rather than the party, clearly talking to one of the aunties from the old wedding-hall days. "No, no, he's doing very well for himself now - the hospital project, the one I told you about. Genuinely brilliant, that boy. I always said capability shows itself eventually, given the chance." Saroja caught Dhriti watching her, mid-sentence, and didn't so much as blink, simply continuing the call with the easy confidence of a woman who had, at some point in the last two years, fully rewritten her own history to have always believed exactly what she now believed. Dhriti let her have it. There didn't seem to be any version of pointing it out that would improve the afternoon, and some part of her - larger than she'd have admitted two years ago - was simply glad to hear the sentence said out loud at all, regardless of who was taking credit for having always known it.

A message buzzed against her phone, face-down on the table beside her, and she almost let it wait until she saw the name. Sravanthi - mostly quiet these last two years, the occasional forwarded reel, a like on a photo now and then. *Saw Arjun's birthday post, he's gotten so big. Congratulations to you both.* A second message followed a minute later, sent, it seemed, with some effort. *For what it's worth - you were right, back at that dinner. I picked the sure thing in the end. He's fine. It's fine.* Dhriti read it twice and felt no urge at all to make a point of it. She typed back only *I'm glad you're okay, Sravanthi*, which was true, and which was, she thought, setting the phone face-down again, the only part of that whole old argument that had ever actually mattered.

Vijay arrived late, as he always did now, trailing apologies about a hospital meeting that had run long, and swept Arjun up into the air the moment he spotted him, drawing a delighted shriek that turned every head on the terrace. "The whole reason any of this worked," he announced to nobody in particular, settling Arjun onto his hip, "is that this one's father listened to me exactly once, and it happened to be the one time that mattered."

"You've told that story at every birthday for two years," Sahishnu said.

"It gets better with repetition."

Manish came in not long after, Priya at his side - the first time Dhriti had ever actually seen her, after two years of hearing the name kept carefully out of rooms. She caught the gold at Priya's throat before she caught anything else about her, and didn't need telling what it meant. "My wife," Manish said, introducing her to a cousin near the door, easily, like the word had never once been the hardest thing he owned. Dhriti caught his eye across the terrace and said nothing about it, which was exactly right.

It was, by any measure that mattered, a good life now, settled into a shape none of them had quite dared imagine two years earlier. The AI project - fully deployed across the hospital network, and two others besides, after the results had come in strong enough that word had travelled - had made real money for everyone attached to it, Vijay included, the hospital included, and Sahishnu's own share of it, folded together with his salary, had crossed forty lakh a year the previous quarter - a number that would have sounded, to the man who'd once typed insurance codes into a clunky intake system for three lakh a year, like something from someone else's life entirely. Three lakh, then six, then this. Dhriti's own number had moved too, quietly, the way these things did when nobody was making a production of it - a promotion the year Arjun was born, a second one since, a title now with *senior* in front of it - though she never once brought it up next to his, not because she was hiding it, only because it had never once felt like the part of either of their stories worth keeping score of. Nobody mentioned the number out loud at the party. It didn't need mentioning. It simply sat underneath everything - the terrace itself, the catering nobody had had to argue about affording, the ease with which Saroja now discussed Sahishnu's work to her own friends, unprompted, in a tone that had nothing defensive left in it at all.

---

The party wound down by evening, guests filtering out in twos and threes, Arjun finally worn down enough by his own birthday to fall asleep mid-sentence in Sahishnu's arms, still faintly sticky with icing. Dhriti carried him to bed herself, tucked him in, stood in the doorway a moment watching him sleep the particular boneless sleep of a two-year-old who'd had an enormous day, and then went back out to find Sahishnu on the terrace, alone now, sitting among the lopsided balloons with two cups of tea he'd made without being asked.

She sat beside him. For a while, neither of them said anything, the evening settling into the comfortable quiet of two people who'd stopped needing to fill every silence.

"Can I ask you something," Sahishnu said eventually. "I've wanted to ask for two years and never quite found the right moment."

"Ask."

"When did you actually decide. About the pregnancy - not that it happened, obviously that wasn't something you decided on your own, but the rest of it. Using it. Telling your mother when you did, the way you did." He turned his cup slowly, not quite looking at her, the same careful gesture she remembered from a hundred small conversations over the years, a man weighing a true thing before saying it out loud. "I've never asked, because I wasn't sure I wanted the answer to change how I felt about any of it. But I think I do want to know now."

Dhriti considered the question for a moment, though she already knew the answer; she'd known it for two years, in fact, exactly as clearly as the day it had happened.

"Kartik," she said. "The afternoon he came for tea. He said something - almost word for word what my mother would have said, the same argument, the same tone, like he'd rehearsed it from listening to her rather than actually thinking it through himself. That was the moment. Not before that. I warned him, plainly, and he laughed at me, and something in me just - decided, right there, that I wasn't going to keep fighting my mother's theory with arguments alone. I was going to prove it wrong with something she couldn't argue with."

"You decided to have a child. As an argument."

"I decided I wanted a life with you, and that I was done waiting for anyone's permission to have it." She said it plainly, no defensiveness in it, though she watched him take the distinction in and turn it over for himself. "The child wasn't a tactic, Sahi. I want you to hear that clearly. But the timing, the way I used it once it was real - yes. That part was deliberate. I'm not going to pretend otherwise to you, of all people."

He was quiet for a long moment, and she let him have the silence, watching something move behind his eyes that she recognised - not anger, she didn't think, but something closer to a man recalibrating a piece of the woman he loved that he'd suspected, distantly, for two years, and was only now hearing confirmed outright.

"You're not a normal woman, Dhriti," he said finally, and there was warmth in it, genuine warmth, but something else underneath the warmth too - a small, honest weight that hadn't fully lifted even now, two years and a settled life later. "I mean that as a compliment. Mostly. I also mean it as - I don't know. I don't think I'd have had the nerve to do what you did. I'm not sure I'm entirely at peace with how far you were willing to go, even now, even knowing how it ended."

"I know you're not." She reached over and took his hand, not to argue the point, just to hold it. "I'm not asking you to be entirely at peace with it. I'm asking you to know the whole truth of who you married, instead of a softer version I've edited down for your comfort. You deserve the whole one."

He turned his hand over in hers, lacing their fingers together, and something in his face settled - not fully resolved, she thought, watching him, but settled enough, the particular peace of a man who'd rather carry an honest complication than a comfortable lie.

"For the record," Dhriti said, after a moment, the corner of her mouth lifting, "I wasn't trying to murder a suitor. I know how that sounds, put together the way you just heard it. I was trying to share a life with you. I just couldn't find a version of convincing my parents that didn't end up looking, from the outside, exactly like a woman trying to murder a suitor."

Sahishnu laughed - properly, the sound building slowly the way it always had, the same laugh from a restaurant table years ago that had once needed permission to exist and clearly didn't anymore. "That's a genuinely terrible thing to joke about."

"I'm aware. I'm choosing to joke about it anyway."

"Of course you are." He was still smiling, shaking his head slightly, and pulled her hand up to press a kiss against her knuckles, unhurried, easy. "I love you. Completely. Even the parts of you I'm still catching up to."

"Good," Dhriti said. "Because there's more where that came from."

The terrace lights buzzed faintly overhead, one of the lopsided balloons finally giving up and drifting down to rest against the railing, and the two of them sat there a while longer in the settled quiet of an ordinary evening, the whole improbable distance between a restaurant hypothetical and this terrace sitting behind them now, folded quietly into something that no longer needed explaining to anyone, least of all to each other.
