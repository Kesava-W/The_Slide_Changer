# Chapter 2 — The Pitch

Sahishnu had failed the same exam three times before anyone in his family stopped mentioning it out loud.

Not failed, exactly - that was the word his relatives reached for, the word that made the story simpler than it was. He'd cleared it eventually, on the third attempt, with a rank that got him a seat nobody at a wedding would ask a follow-up question about. Two drop years sat behind that rank, and behind the drop years sat a truth he'd made peace with earlier than anyone expected him to: he wasn't slow. He just didn't learn from a page the way the exam wanted him to. Put a circuit in front of him, or a half-built engine, or - later - a laptop with a broken script open on it, and something in his head found its way in through the back door, no coaching class required. Put a textbook chapter in front of him and ask him to reproduce it under a clock, and he lost, reliably - a format he never found his footing in, no matter how many times he tried.

He never once told anyone the exam was unfair, even when other repeaters around him did. It had just never felt like his to say. If the exam measured something and he came up short on it, the shortcoming was the honest description. B.Tech at twenty-three, CGPA six-five, a number he could recite without flinching because flinching would have meant believing it needed defending.

Vijay never asked him to defend it.

They'd known each other since school, long before either of the drop years, in the loose way that survives sporadic despite two very different trajectories - Vijay through medical college on a straight, unbroken line, Sahishnu through two extra years and a degree that opened fewer doors than it was supposed to. They still met when Vijay was back in Hyderabad, usually at the same tea stall near Vijay's hostel, usually with Vijay doing most of the talking, which had always suited Sahishnu fine.

"I need you for something," Vijay said, the week Sahishnu's final semester results came out. No hello first. That was also very Vijay.

"You need me for something," Sahishnu repeated, stirring sugar into chai he hadn't asked to be this sweet.

"The hospital's agreed to fund it. Actual funding, rey - not 'let's see how it goes,' actual money, and they want co-ownership on whatever comes out of it, which means they're serious." Vijay was talking fast, the way he did when an idea had been sitting in him for months and finally found an audience. "AI. In healthcare. Patient records, eventually diagnostics support - I've been pitching this to anyone who'd sit still for it since my first year of residency, and the hospital administration finally said yes."

"You're a second-year resident."

"I'm aware."

"Building AI software."

"Not building it myself, obviously - that's you. I know what the disease looks like. I don't know what a neural network looks like. You do."

Sahishnu set his cup down. "I don't, actually. Not properly. I've messed around with some of it - tutorials, a couple of half-finished things on my laptop that nobody's ever seen because they're not finished. That's not the same as building something a hospital is going to put real patients' records into."

"So learn the rest on the way. You've never once needed the classroom version of anything, that's the whole point of asking you and not one of the toppers from our batch." Vijay said it like a compliment, and it took Sahishnu a second to realise that, from Vijay, it actually was one. "I've watched you take apart things you had no business understanding since we were fifteen. This is just a bigger thing to take apart."

Sahishnu didn't answer right away. Outside the tea stall, an auto went past with its horn stuck on, someone shouting at the driver to fix it or park it. He watched it go, mostly so he wouldn't have to look at Vijay while he thought.

The honest shape of his hesitation wasn't about the technology. It was that Vijay's confidence in him had always run ahead of his own, by a margin that made him faintly uneasy - the way it feels to be handed something heavier than you offered to carry. Six-five CGPA. Two drop years. A track record, on paper, of exactly the kind of person hospitals did not hand responsibility to. If he said yes and it went wrong, it wouldn't just be his failure. It would confirm every quiet assumption anyone had ever made about him, this time with Vijay's name attached to it too.

"If I get this wrong," Sahishnu said, "it's not just my name on it."

"I know that better than you do. It's literally my funding." Vijay leaned back, entirely unbothered, which was somehow the most convincing argument he'd made all evening. "I'm not offering you this because I feel sorry for you, or because we've known each other since we were kids and I owe you one. I'm offering it because I actually think you're the right person for it. Those aren't the same thing, and I need you to hear the difference."

Sahishnu looked at him for a long moment. It wasn't agreement yet, not out loud - but something in his chest had already started leaning toward yes, the way it always did once Vijay had said a thing directly enough that there was nowhere left to hide from it.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay?"

"Okay. I'm in." He said it quietly, the way he said most things, but something had shifted in his face that Vijay clearly caught, because Vijay grinned in the exact way he only did when he'd won an argument he'd already assumed he'd win.

---

The first weeks were nothing like the tea-stall pitch had made them sound. There was no grand unveiling, no hospital boardroom moment - just a shared laptop, a cramped side office the hospital had given them that used to store old files, and a mounting pile of documentation Sahishnu read three times each because the language of it, formal and clinical, fought him the same way his textbooks always had.

But underneath the fighting, something else was happening, and it took him almost two weeks to notice it: he wasn't dreading the mornings.

He'd get to the office before Vijay most days, laptop open, coffee going cold beside him, and lose an hour without noticing it had passed - not because the work was easy, it wasn't, but because for once the difficulty didn't feel like a verdict on him. A model that wouldn't converge was a problem to be taken apart, the same as a stuck circuit at fifteen, the same as anything he'd ever actually been good at. Nobody was timing him. Nobody was grading him against anyone else at all.

There was a night, ten days in, when the model kept collapsing to the same useless output no matter what he fed it, and he stayed past nine trying variations nobody had asked him to try, not because Vijay was watching but because the wrongness of it had lodged somewhere he couldn't leave alone until it was fixed. He found it near midnight - a scaling error in how the patient vitals were being normalised, nothing exotic, just something he'd missed on the first four passes and caught on the fifth because he refused to stop looking. He didn't call Vijay to tell him. He wrote it up in two flat lines and left it for the morning, the way you'd leave a note for someone rather than wake them with good news that could wait.

Vijay read the note before Sahishnu had even taken his shoes off the next day and looked up at him like he'd been half-expecting it. "Told you," he said, for what would become the first of many times, and went back to his coffee, unsurprised in a way that Sahishnu found he didn't mind.

Vijay noticed the pattern too, in his own way - stopped double-checking Sahishnu's explanations after the first fortnight, started bringing him straight into meetings with the hospital's IT liaison instead of briefing him after the fact. It wasn't said out loud between them. Vijay wasn't the kind of friend who narrated his own trust; he just extended more of it, a little at a time, and let Sahishnu notice on his own that it had happened.

A few weeks further in, packing up late, Vijay tossed him a bottle of water and said it a third time, almost as an aside - "Told you" - already halfway out the door, not waiting for a response this time either.

Sahishnu let him go without answering. He sat there a moment longer after Vijay left, laptop still open, the model still running its last epoch of the night, and let himself feel - carefully, like testing a bruise - something close to the specific relief of being useful in a way that had nothing to do with a rank list.

It wasn't pride, not yet. He wasn't there yet. But it was the first job he'd ever had where the number attached to him - six-five, three attempts, two years lost - hadn't walked into the room ahead of him.
