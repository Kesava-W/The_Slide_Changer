# Chapter 16 — Power Cut

Two days later.

Her parents left before noon, an overnight bag packed light, off to sit with a family friend who'd been admitted for a minor procedure that had turned into a longer hospital stay than anyone expected. Saroja had left instructions about the fridge and a number to call if anything came up, and then the house had gone quiet in the particular way it only went quiet when it was genuinely empty, not just softened by the ordinary hush of a weekday afternoon.

Dhriti sat with that quiet for a while after they'd gone, turning her phone over in her hand. Sahishnu had mentioned, a few days back, that his parents were travelling to his mother's village for some relative's function - back Thursday, he'd said, an ordinary throwaway detail from an ordinary phone call, filed away without either of them making anything of it at the time.

She thought about it now for longer than the detail probably deserved.

She texted him a little after two. *My parents are out of town tonight.*

*Mine too, actually,* he wrote back, a few minutes later. *Small world.*

*Small world,* she sent back, and left it there, neither of them saying the next obvious sentence out loud, though she suspected, reading his reply twice over, that he'd understood exactly what she was leaving unsaid.

*Come over if you want,* he wrote, a few minutes later, the kind of message that had clearly taken him longer to send than the length of it suggested. *No pressure. Just - if you want.*

*I want,* she typed back, then deleted it, then typed it again exactly the same way and sent it before she could talk herself into something more careful.

She got ready slowly that evening, unhurried, and told herself the whole time that she wasn't deciding anything, only that a free evening and an empty house both happened to be true at once, which wasn't the same thing as a plan. The auto ride over took longer than usual, some diversion near the flyover, and she spent most of it looking out at the ordinary evening traffic, streetlights coming on one by one, a city entirely indifferent to the particular evening it happened to be for her.

---

His flat looked different with nobody else in it - quieter than she'd seen it, the two flatmates apparently out for the evening as well, a coincidence Sahishnu mentioned with a slightly self-conscious laugh when he opened the door, like he wasn't sure whether to be glad about it or embarrassed by how glad he was.

"I made chai," he said, stepping back to let her in. "Badly, probably. I never really learned properly."

"I'll be the judge of that."

The chai was, in fact, slightly over-boiled, and she told him so, and he laughed - properly, easily, none of the careful weight that had sat between them in the months after the park, that particular caution both of them had been quietly working to unlearn ever since. He'd tidied the flat more than usual, she noticed, a stack of forms that normally lived on the one good chair now squared away somewhere out of sight, and she didn't say anything about noticing, letting him have the small, private effort of it.

They sat together on the narrow sofa, the evening settling around them unhurried, talking about nothing in particular the way people talk when the talking itself isn't really the point - his day, a minor disaster with a deployment script, something Ramana had said in passing that he still thought about sometimes even though he hadn't seen the man in months. He showed her, at one point, a photo on his phone from the old hospital project, the two-year-old picture she'd first seen him in, and told her properly, for the first time, who everyone in it was - a running joke about the doctor third from left, a story about the whiteboard behind them that had taken four different colours of marker and three arguments to finish.

"You never actually told me the story behind this photo," she said. "I've had it memorised for almost a year and you never once asked why."

"I assumed you just liked looking at old photos of me."

"I do. That's not the point."

He smiled at that, the specific smile she'd come to think of as entirely hers, and didn't ask what had made her bring it up now, of all evenings, though something in his face suggested he might have guessed anyway and decided not to press it.

At some point the conversation thinned out into something quieter, comfortable in a way that didn't need filling, and she found herself closer to him on the small sofa than either of them had consciously arranged, his arm along the back of it, not quite around her, not quite not.

"I like this," he said, quietly, not looking at her directly, the particular carefulness he brought to saying true things out loud. "Just this. Ordinary evening. Nobody watching."

"Me too."

He turned to look at her properly then, something unguarded in his face that she'd come to recognise as entirely hers, a version of him nobody else ever got to see, and the space between them closed the rest of the way without either of them deciding it in words.

The lights went out perhaps ten minutes later, the whole building dropping into sudden dark at once - a scheduled outage neither of them had checked for, or simple bad luck, it didn't matter which. Somewhere below, a neighbour groaned theatrically into the stairwell. A dog barked twice, near the compound wall, then settled.

Neither of them moved to find a candle.

The city outside kept going without them - a horn somewhere on the main road, a television murmuring through a neighbour's wall, the ordinary, indifferent noise of Hyderabad on a Tuesday night that had no idea what it was covering for. The dark held. Nobody switched anything back on for a long time.
