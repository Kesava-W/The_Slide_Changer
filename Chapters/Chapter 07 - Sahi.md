# Chapter 7 — Sahi

He was already there when she arrived, which she hadn't expected - most men she'd met through this particular circuit of friends-of-friends showed up seven, eight minutes late, as if punctuality were a currency to be spent carefully rather than a simple courtesy. Sahishnu was standing outside the café, not sitting, not scrolling his phone to look occupied, just standing there with his hands in his pockets, watching the street with the specific patience of someone who didn't mind waiting because waiting cost him nothing.

"You didn't have to wait outside," she said, coming up beside him. "I'm not late."

"I know. I was early." He said it simply, no apology in it, like being early was just a fact about the evening rather than a confession. "Wanted to get the table by the window before someone else did. This place fills up fast on weekends."

It was such a small, unglamorous thing to have thought about - not the restaurant, not the outfit, just a window table - that Dhriti found herself unexpectedly charmed by it before either of them had said anything that mattered.

The table, when they sat, did have a window, overlooking a narrow street strung with cable wires and a paanwala's cart directly opposite, and Dhriti found herself glancing out at it more than once over the next two hours, mostly because looking away from Sahishnu occasionally felt necessary, like blinking.

"So," he said, once the waiter had taken their order and left them with nothing to do but actually talk. "Manish tells me you're persistent."

"Manish tells you things about me?"

"One thing. That you don't really do half-measures." He was watching her with an expression she couldn't immediately place - not nervous exactly, though there was something careful underneath it, like a man checking a bridge's weight limit before deciding how much of himself to put on it. "Is that true?"

"Depends what you're asking about." Dhriti turned her water glass a quarter turn, buying herself half a second. "I don't do half-measures about things I've actually decided matter. Most things don't clear that bar. The ones that do, I don't really back off from."

"And I cleared the bar."

"You're sitting here, aren't you."

He laughed at that - short, surprised, the kind of laugh that seemed to catch him slightly off guard, like he hadn't expected to actually laugh tonight and was recalibrating in real time. "Fair," he said. "Fair enough."

They talked for a while about nothing that mattered and everything that did - her job, sanded down carefully around the one detail that wasn't true, though she found she didn't have to lie much beyond that single number; the rest of her actual life held up fine without embellishment. He talked about the data entry job with a dry, self-deprecating honesty that surprised her, no attempt to dress it up as more than it was, and something in the way he described it - "I type things into boxes all day, it's not complicated, I just happen to be very fast at it now" - made her want to ask about the two years before it, the hospital project, the thing she'd actually seen the photo from in the first place.

"Someone told me you built something for a hospital once," she said carefully. "AI, patient records, something like that."

Something crossed his face - not closing off exactly, but a visible decision about how much to let show. "Tried to. It didn't survive the funding, in the end. Two years of work, and then one email and it's just - gone." He said it evenly, no self-pity in it, which she found she believed more than she'd have believed a more dramatic version. "I don't mind talking about it. Most people change the subject faster than I would, once they hear 'the project failed.' You didn't."

"Should I have?"

"No." He looked at her properly then, something in his expression settling, like a decision he'd been holding off making had just resolved itself. "I'd rather people ask the real question than tiptoe around a smaller, kinder one."

Dinner came and went slower than either of them seemed to notice, the paanwala's cart outside doing brisk business under a single bare bulb, the restaurant thinning out around them as later tables cleared without either of them registering it. She made him laugh twice more - once at an entirely unfair impression of her HR director's meeting voice, once at a story about Meghana eating an entire table's worth of naan under the pretense of "helping finish it" - and found, with some surprise, that he had a laugh that started low and built, like he wasn't sure at first whether he was allowed to find something that funny.

"You've got a weirdly specific talent for making fun of people you clearly like," he said, still half-laughing. "Your friends. Your director. Me, probably, eventually."

"You, definitely, eventually."

"Good," he said, and meant it, in a way that made something in her chest do something she hadn't planned for.

It was somewhere past the second cup of coffee that she landed on it - not planned, not rehearsed, just arriving the way the right word sometimes arrives mid-sentence before you've consciously gone looking for it.

"Sahishnu's a mouthful," she said. "Four syllables, every time. I'm going to call you Sahi."

He blinked. "Sahi."

"Short. Efficient. Also means 'correct,' doesn't it - or 'right'? So it works either way. Sahishnu the correct answer." She said it lightly, teasing, not expecting it to land as anything more than a joke, and was mildly startled by what actually crossed his face in response - something unguarded, something that looked, for just a second, like a man who'd spent a long time being told, in various quiet ways, that he was the wrong answer to most questions, hearing the opposite out loud for what might have been the first time in longer than he'd admit to.

"Nobody's called me that before," he said, and there was something in his voice that had gone slightly rougher than the rest of the evening, like the joke had reached somewhere the rest of the conversation hadn't quite managed to.

"Well. Now someone has."

"Sahi," he repeated, testing it, and something about hearing him say his own new name back to her, half-amused and half something else she didn't have a word for yet, undid a small, careful part of her that had been holding this whole evening at arm's length, evaluating it, keeping score. She stopped keeping score sometime around then. She noticed herself stop and didn't try to start again.

He insisted on walking her to her cab afterward, not in the performed way some men did it, checking off a box they thought counted for something, but like it hadn't occurred to him to do otherwise. The street had gone quieter, the paanwala packing up his cart for the night, a stray dog asleep against the base of a shuttered shop.

"I had a good time," he said, hands back in his pockets, the same stance he'd been standing in when she'd arrived, like the evening had folded back on itself. "I wasn't expecting to. I mean - I was hoping to, obviously, but hoping and expecting aren't the same thing."

"You sound surprised you're allowed to have a good time."

"Maybe a little." He said it lightly, but there was something honest underneath the lightness, an admission he hadn't fully meant to make out loud. "The last couple of years haven't given me a lot of practice at it."

"Well." She opened the cab door herself before he could reach for it, which made him huff something that wasn't quite a laugh. "Get some more practice, then. I'm told I'm quite persistent."

"So I've heard." He was smiling properly now, none of the earlier carefulness left in it. "Goodnight, Dhriti."

"Goodnight, Sahi."

He said something back to that - she caught only the shape of it through the closing door, not the words, but the tone of it stayed with her the whole ride home, something warm and slightly disbelieving, like a man testing whether a piece of good luck was going to hold if he looked at it too directly.

---

She got home later than she'd told her parents she would, letting herself in quietly, the house dark except for the kitchen light her mother always left on for her. She stood in the hallway for a moment, replaying the evening in pieces - the window table, the paanwala's cart, his laugh building slowly like it needed permission, the way he'd said Sahi like he was trying it on for size and finding, to his own visible surprise, that it fit.

She was happy. It sat in her plainly, without complication, for exactly as long as it took her to reach her room and close the door behind her.

Then the other thought arrived, the one she'd been keeping at a careful distance all evening without quite admitting she was doing it - that everything he'd said tonight, every honest, unguarded thing, every laugh that had built slowly like it needed permission, he'd said to a version of her that didn't fully exist. Receptionist. Comparable salary. A woman he could sit across from without doing the quiet arithmetic she'd watched him almost do, once, when she'd mentioned in passing how her office handled year-end bonuses, before he'd visibly caught himself and let it go.

She didn't know yet what she'd do when the gap closed - only that it would, eventually, because gaps like that always did, on their own schedule and not hers. She sat on the edge of her bed a long while, still in the evening's clothes, turning the problem over without arriving anywhere close to an answer.

She fell asleep before she found one. She didn't stop looking, even so.
