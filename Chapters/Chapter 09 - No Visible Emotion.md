# Chapter 9 — No Visible Emotion

The email came through the usual channel, three days out: *Mandatory training - Data Handling & Compliance Refresher - all data entry operators - Conference Room B, 10 AM.* Sahishnu read it the way he read most internal emails, which was to say barely, and forwarded it to nobody, because nobody needed forwarding on a Tuesday training session that would almost certainly run long and teach him nothing he didn't already know.

Ramana groaned about it over coffee that morning. "HR again. Third one this quarter."

"At least it's not the fire safety one," Sahishnu said. "That one had the video."

"God, the video." Ramana shuddered, theatrically, and the two of them filed into Conference Room B with the rest of the operators at five to ten, taking seats near the back out of the same habit that put students near the back of any classroom that didn't require anything of them.

The projector was already on when he sat down, throwing a title slide onto the screen at the front - blue and white, corporate, a company logo he half-recognised from somewhere he couldn't immediately place. A woman stood beside the laptop at the front of the room, back to him, adjusting the screen angle, saying something to a colleague he couldn't hear over the room's settling noise.

She turned around.

He didn't move. That was the first thing his body decided, before his mind had caught up to deciding anything at all - stillness, total and immediate, the kind of stillness that in a different context might have looked like calm.

It was her.

Not a resemblance. Not a trick of the projector light or a woman who happened to share her build, her laugh line, the particular way she tucked a strand of hair back when she was about to start talking. It was Dhriti, standing at the front of Conference Room B in a blazer he'd never seen her wear, a lanyard around her neck with a company ID he was too far away to read clearly. He didn't need to read it to know it wasn't the small, unremarkable firm Manish had named him three months ago. A receptionist didn't run a compliance training for a room of forty people. A receptionist didn't own a room like she was owning this one.

"Good morning, everyone," she said, in a voice he knew, pitched slightly differently than he'd ever heard it - flatter, more practised, a work voice, a voice built for exactly this room and exactly this audience, and somewhere underneath the ringing in his own ears he registered, distantly, that she hadn't seen him yet, was still scanning the room the way a presenter scans a room, without landing anywhere in particular.

Then she did land. On him.

Her face didn't change. That was the thing he'd think about later, replaying it - how completely her face didn't change, not a flicker, not the smallest crack in the professional composure she was wearing like the blazer, and some cold, clear part of his mind noted that a person didn't get that good at not reacting by accident. She'd had practice. He didn't yet know at what.

"We'll be covering three areas today," she said, turning to the laptop, and the first slide changed behind her - a title card giving way to a bulleted list, *Data Classification*, *Access Protocols*, *Incident Reporting* - and Sahishnu watched her hand on the trackpad change it, a small, ordinary gesture, the kind of thing a hundred presenters did in a hundred rooms every day, and felt something in his chest fold in on itself very quietly, without any outward sign that it was happening at all.

*She works reception. Salary's close to mine.*

That was the sentence Manish had handed him, three months ago, and he'd built an entire spring and summer on top of it without once checking the foundation, because why would he - because she'd never given him a single reason to check, because every detail she'd ever offered him about her days, her office, her frustrations with a difficult manager, had slotted into that sentence without a seam showing anywhere.

He started running it backward now, in real time, while she talked about data classification tiers to a room of people who had no idea what was happening two rows from the front. The headphones. He'd thought, at the time, *that's a generous sale*, and let it go, because letting things go was easier than making someone he - than making someone feel accused over a gift. The restaurant where she'd insisted on paying for the auto both ways, small enough that he couldn't build an argument out of refusing it. The bonus comment, months back, that she'd let slip and then visibly walked back, and he'd caught the walk-back at the time and decided not to ask, because asking would have meant admitting he was keeping score, and he hadn't wanted to be someone who kept score with her.

There was the evening, too, three weeks back, when her phone had rung mid-dinner and she'd stepped outside to take it, coming back in eight minutes later with an offhand *sorry, work thing, someone's reporting manager being difficult about a reimbursement* - a sentence that had made perfect sense at the time, the small, forgettable friction of a reception desk fielding someone else's problem, and made an entirely different kind of sense now, watching her stand at the front of a compliance training with the easy authority of someone who resolved exactly that category of problem for a living, at a level several rungs above anyone who sat behind a front desk.

Every one of those moments had had a shape he could see now, laid end to end, that he hadn't let himself see at the time. He'd had the evidence. He'd simply wanted the other version of the story badly enough not to look at it too closely.

"- and any exceptions should be routed through your team lead before end of day," Dhriti was saying, another slide changing behind her, and Sahishnu became aware, distantly, that Ramana had said something to him and was waiting for a response.

"Sorry - what?"

"I said this is actually not the worst one. She's better than the last presenter." Ramana nodded toward the front, entirely unaware of what the sentence had just done, walking casually across a floor that had opened up beneath Sahishnu without making a sound. "You look terrible, man. You good?"

"Fine," Sahishnu said. "Didn't sleep well."

It came out level. He heard it come out level, heard his own voice doing the thing his face was also apparently doing - holding, absorbing, giving nothing away that a room full of half-attentive colleagues would notice - and some part of him, watching himself do it, understood that he'd had a great deal of practice at exactly this particular skill, for exactly the opposite reason he was using it now. He'd learned to keep his face still over a rank list. He'd learned to keep it still over a funding email. This was, at least, familiar ground, even if the thing walking across it was new.

Their eyes met twice more over the next forty minutes. Once when she glanced up from the laptop mid-sentence, scanning the room the way presenters did to check the audience was still with her, and found him without seeming to search for him, her expression giving away exactly as much as the first time - nothing, or nothing anyone else in the room would have caught. Once more near the end, when a slide changed to a summary screen and she looked directly at him for perhaps two full seconds, long enough that it stopped being incidental and started being a question neither of them was going to ask out loud in Conference Room B.

He didn't look away first. He wanted that, afterward, to count for something, even if he wasn't sure yet what.

There was one moment, somewhere in the middle third of the session, when her voice caught - not dramatically, nothing anyone else in the room would have registered as anything but a presenter clearing her throat mid-sentence, reaching for a glass of water that wasn't strictly necessary. He knew what he'd just watched, even so. He'd built three months of a relationship on being able to read the small print under her composure, the tells nobody else got access to, and he read this one clearly enough - she'd seen his face do nothing, and the nothing had cost her something to watch.

He took no satisfaction in that. He noted it, filed it away with everything else he was filing away that morning, and kept his own face exactly where it had been since she'd turned around.

"Any questions before we wrap up?" Dhriti asked the room, and there were two - mundane ones, about form retention periods, easily answered - and then it was over, chairs scraping back, operators filing out in twos and threes back toward the long room of desks, the rain-on-tin-roof sound of typing waiting to swallow the morning back into ordinary shape.

Sahishnu stood, gathered his things with hands that felt like they belonged to someone else, and made it almost to the door before she started moving toward him from the front of the room, not hurrying, but clearly, deliberately, closing the distance.

He didn't let her close it.

"Sahishnu-"

"I'm not feeling well," he said, not looking at her directly, aimed somewhere just past her shoulder, at the door, at the ordinary hallway beyond it that suddenly looked like the only safe place left in the building. "Think I'm coming down with something. I should head home."

"Sahishnu, can we just-"

"I'll see you," he said, which wasn't a real sentence, wasn't an answer to anything, and he heard himself say it and kept walking anyway, out through the door, down the corridor, past Ramana calling something after him he didn't catch, out into the parking lot where the midday heat hit him like something solid.

He didn't stop walking until he reached the bus stand, two streets down, further than he needed to go for any bus that actually served him. He stood there a while, not looking at his phone, not looking at anything in particular, running the last three months backward one more time from the beginning, more carefully now, looking for the parts he'd have caught if he'd wanted to catch them.

He found several. He didn't examine any of them for long. There would be time for that later, alone, somewhere that wasn't a bus stand two streets from the office he'd sat in that morning without any idea what it was about to cost him, in a city that had just, without any warning at all, become considerably smaller than it had been a few hours ago.

His face, the whole walk there, gave away nothing. Nobody who passed him would have looked twice.
