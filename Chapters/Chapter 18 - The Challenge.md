# Chapter 18 — The Challenge

Saroja had the date nearly fixed by the second week - a muhurtham in late November, a hall already shortlisted, Kartik's mother apparently eager enough to move fast that the whole machinery of it had picked up a momentum Dhriti hadn't quite managed to slow down without simply refusing outright, which she wasn't ready to do yet, not until she had something that would end it cleanly rather than just delay it.

She chose the evening carefully. Her parents were both home, dinner cleared, her mother going through wedding-hall brochures at the dining table with the particular pleasure of a woman finally getting the outcome she'd been steering toward for months.

"I spoke to Kartik's mother today," Saroja said, not looking up from the brochure. "They'd like to fix the engagement date within the month, if we're agreeable. I don't see a reason to drag this out, do you?"

"I do, actually." Dhriti stood in the doorway, not sitting down, some part of her recognising that she needed to say this standing, on her own feet, not folded into a chair across the table like a woman asking permission. "I'm not marrying Kartik, Amma."

Saroja set the brochure down, slow, the particular stillness of someone who'd heard this exact sentence from her daughter before and had decided, apparently, not to take it seriously this time either. "We've discussed this."

"We haven't discussed this. You've told me what you want, and I've told you I'm not doing it, and you've kept moving forward anyway like my answer doesn't count." Dhriti kept her voice level, though something in her chest had gone tight and cold, the specific feeling of standing at the edge of a decision she'd already made weeks ago and was only now letting herself fully arrive at. "I need you to actually hear me this time. I'm not marrying him. I can't marry him."

"Can't." Saroja's voice sharpened. "What does *can't* mean, Dhriti."

Dhriti held her mother's gaze and didn't soften any of it. "I'm pregnant. Ten weeks along. It's Sahishnu's."

The dining room went very still.

Prakash's teacup, halfway to his mouth, stopped there, suspended, his face doing something complicated - not anger, not yet, something closer to a man watching the floor of his own house shift under him without warning. Saroja didn't move at all for a long moment, and when she did, it wasn't the explosion Dhriti had braced for. It was smaller than that, and somehow worse - her mother's face draining of something, the wedding-hall brochure still open under her hand, forgotten now, the whole careful architecture of the last two weeks collapsing in real time behind her eyes.

"Say that again," Saroja said, very quietly.

"I'm pregnant. Sahishnu's the father. I'm not marrying Kartik, and I'm not going to pretend this hasn't already been decided, because it has - by me, weeks ago, and I should have told you sooner instead of letting you plan a wedding I was never going to attend."

"You let me - " Saroja's voice cracked on the sentence, something genuinely wounded underneath the anger now, and Dhriti watched her mother's hand come up to her own mouth, briefly, an old, unconscious gesture Dhriti hadn't seen from her in years, not since a death in the family long before Dhriti was old enough to fully understand it. "You let me sit here choosing a hall. You let me call that boy's mother. You knew, this whole time, and you let me make a fool of myself in front of that family."

"I did." Dhriti didn't flinch from it, didn't soften it, because softening it now would have been its own kind of lie, and she'd decided, somewhere in the last two months, that she was done managing her mother's feelings around a decision that had never really been her mother's to approve. "I'm not proud of the timing. I am not sorry about the decision."

"Dhriti." Prakash's voice, finally, quiet, the teacup set down now with a hand that wasn't quite steady. "Unmarried. Pregnant. Do you understand what you're saying, what this means for-"

"I understand exactly what it means, Nanna. I've understood it since the test came back positive." She turned to him, and something in her softened slightly, the way it always did around her father, even now. "I know what people will say. I know what this costs, in this family, in this city. I made the decision anyway, with my eyes open, because the alternative was marrying a man who thinks security and salary are the same thing, watching my daughter or son grow up inside that same belief, and I would rather face every single thing people are about to say about me than live that life instead."

"So ask him, then." Dhriti turned back to her mother, and something in her voice had gone very still now, very deliberate, the particular calm she reached for only when she'd already made peace with whatever came next. "Go ahead. Call Kartik's mother back tomorrow. Tell them the wedding's still on. See how fast that family finds a reason to withdraw once they hear what I've just told you. I'd genuinely like to watch you try."

"Dhriti, this isn't a game-"

"I'm not playing a game, Amma. I'm telling you the truth, which is more than this family's been willing to sit with from me in months." Dhriti held her mother's eyes, unflinching, and let the silence after the sentence do the rest of the work rather than filling it with anything softer. "You wanted proof that salary isn't security. Here it is. A man earning three times what Sahishnu makes is going to run the moment he hears this news, and Sahishnu isn't going to run at all. Watch both of those things happen, and then tell me again which one of us had the wrong theory."

Saroja stared at her for a long moment, something in her face that Dhriti had never seen there before - not fury exactly, though the fury was in it too, but something closer to grief, a mother watching a version of her daughter she hadn't fully believed existed until this exact moment, standing in her dining room, entirely unapologetic about a decision that had just upended everything Saroja had spent months building.

"I don't know who you are right now," Saroja said, quietly, and turned toward the door, then stopped, her back still to Dhriti, her shoulders held in a way that suggested the sentence wasn't quite finished. "I was trying to make sure nobody could ever look at you the way people looked at my own mother, after my father died and left her with nothing but a house and four daughters to marry off on no income at all. I don't expect you to understand that. I don't think I've ever actually told you that." Her voice had gone rough on the last of it, something old and unhealed surfacing briefly before she visibly closed it back over. "I still don't know who you are right now."

She left the room without another word, the brochure still open on the table behind her.

Prakash sat there a while longer, silent, and when he finally looked up at Dhriti, his face held none of his wife's anger - just a kind of stunned, careful bewilderment, a father trying to reconcile the daughter he thought he knew with the one currently standing in his dining room having just detonated her own future without a visible tremor in her voice.

"Was there truly no other way," he asked, "than this?"

"I don't know," Dhriti said, honestly, the first uncertain thing she'd allowed herself to say all evening. "I only know I couldn't find one in time. I'm sorry it cost you both like this. I'm not sorry about the child, or about Sahishnu. I need you to understand the difference."

He didn't answer that, not right away, just looked at her a while longer with an expression she couldn't fully read, and Dhriti let him have the silence, understanding that he needed it more than he needed anything she could say to fill it.

---

Kartik's mother called two days later. Dhriti wasn't in the room when Saroja took the call, but she heard enough of it through the wall - her mother's voice going tight, formal, apologetic in the particular register reserved for conversations you're ending rather than continuing, something about *unforeseen circumstances* and *wishing the family well* - and understood, without needing to be told directly, exactly how the conversation had gone.

Kartik hadn't called her himself. She hadn't expected him to. He'd made his position clear enough over tea on that balcony months ago, and she suspected, picturing his face receiving the news secondhand through his mother, that the laugh he'd given her that afternoon had curdled into something considerably less amused.

She didn't feel triumphant about it, not exactly. She felt, mostly, the particular quiet of a bet she'd made a long time ago finally, fully proving itself, at a cost she was only now beginning to properly measure - her mother's face in the dining room, her father's careful, unanswered question, a family dinner table that would take a long time to feel ordinary again.

She thought it had been worth it. She kept thinking that, deliberately, every time the cost of it threatened to outweigh the certainty.
