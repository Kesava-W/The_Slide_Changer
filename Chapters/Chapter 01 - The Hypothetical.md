# Chapter 1 — The Hypothetical

The restaurant sat on the seventeenth floor of a glass tower off Banjara Hills, the kind of place where the menu didn't print prices next to the starters, only next to the mains, as if asking felt ruder than the cost itself. Dhriti had booked the table three days in advance under her own name, because someone had to, and because she liked being the one who did.

"To Ankita's promotion," Sravanthi said, raising her glass, "and to none of us having to split the bill by UPI screenshots for once."

Five glasses went up. Dhriti's included. Around her sat the same five faces that had sat around some table or other since their MBA batch scattered into five different companies and, in two cases, two different cities and back - Ankita in consulting now, senior enough that the promotion had actually meant something; Meghana still finishing her PG in radiology, perpetually exhausted, perpetually the one who ordered the least and ate the most off everyone else's plates; Sravanthi in a bank, two years into a role she complained about with the settled fondness of someone who wasn't actually looking to leave.

Between them they covered most of the ways a woman in her mid-twenties in this city could be living - Meghana still in a girls' PG near her hospital, sharing a room and a geyser schedule with a stranger who'd become something like a sister by attrition; Ankita in a rented 2BHK with a flatmate she never saw except on the electricity bill; Dhriti the only one of the five still at her parents' house in Jubilee Hills, not because she couldn't afford to leave but because nobody had yet given her a reason good enough to.

The starters kept arriving faster than they were eaten - patched together across four orders, apologised for and unrepented in the same breath. Somewhere in the middle of it Ankita's promotion got its second toast, unprompted, because Meghana insisted the first one "didn't count, nobody was listening."

Dhriti didn't say much for the first twenty minutes. She rarely did in the noisy stretch of an evening - the ordering, the reordering, the argument over whether four starters were enough for five people who all insisted they weren't that hungry. She ate a spring roll. She watched Sravanthi check her phone twice, put it face-down, check it again.

"Okay, what," Dhriti said.

"Nothing."

"Sravanthi."

Sravanthi exhaled through her nose, the sound of someone who'd been waiting to be asked. "My mother sent me two more profiles today. From the app - the family one, not the real one."

"And?"

"And I said no to both before she finished the sentence." Sravanthi turned her wine glass by the stem, not drinking from it. "One of them is a manager somewhere, earns maybe six. I'm at eleven now, with the hike. My mother doesn't understand why that's a problem."

"Is it a problem?" Meghana asked, not looking up from the naan she was tearing.

"It's not not a problem." Sravanthi said it flatly, like a rule she'd stopped needing to defend to herself a long time ago. "I'm not going to spend my life explaining an app-based investment to a man who thinks SIP is a typo. I want someone who's already arrived. Is that so unreasonable?"

"You want someone who's already arrived," Dhriti repeated, mostly to herself.

"Don't start."

"I'm not starting anything." Dhriti picked up her water glass. "I just think 'already arrived' is doing a lot of work in that sentence."

Sravanthi set her wine down harder than the moment required. "Fine. Then what would you do? Since apparently I've said something wrong."

The table had gone quiet in the particular way it did when Dhriti was about to make a point - not dramatically, nobody leaned in, but the cutlery noise dropped and Ankita's phone, mid-scroll, stopped scrolling.

"Two men," Dhriti said. "Same age as you, same background, same starting point. One of them earns four lakh less than you right now, but he's sharp - genuinely capable, the kind of person who's underpriced for what he can actually do. Give it two years and he's earning more than you. The other one earns two lakh more than you right now, but it's not really about ability - family connections, timing, a company that overpays for the role. Give it two years and he's slipped behind you, maybe badly. Which one do you marry."

"That's not a real question," Sravanthi said. "You can't know either of those things in advance."

"I didn't ask if you could know it. I asked which one you'd pick, if you had to pick blind."

Sravanthi opened her mouth, then stopped. She picked up her phone as if it might have the answer, put it back down. "Okay - the first one obviously eventually earns more, so-"

"You're doing maths," Dhriti said. "I didn't ask you to do maths."

"You're the one who put numbers in it!"

"The numbers are a detail. I'm asking what you'd actually choose, with your eyes open, before you know how either story ends." Dhriti wasn't smiling exactly, but there was something amused sitting behind her eyes, watching Sravanthi try to solve a problem that wasn't the kind that got solved with arithmetic.

Sravanthi looked to Ankita for help. Ankita held both hands up, laughing - out of this one, thanks. Meghana had actually stopped eating now, which for Meghana was the equivalent of anyone else standing up.

"I don't - " Sravanthi started, then stopped again, genuinely stuck now, not performing it. "I don't know. Tell me what you'd do."

"The first one," Dhriti said, without any pause at all, the kind of answer that had clearly not required the two years everyone else had just spent arriving at.

"The lower earner."

"The capable one. Those aren't the same thing, that's the whole point of the question."

"But you just said he ends up earning more anyway - so you're still picking the guy who gets rich. You just did it the long way round."

"No." Dhriti said it plainly, no heat in it, which somehow made it land harder than if she'd argued. "I'm not picking him because he ends up rich. If he never earned a rupee more than he does now, I'd still pick him over the other one. Richness isn't the reason. It's just what tends to follow, if you picked right in the first place. Capability comes first. The money is just weather after that - you don't marry someone for good weather, you marry them because you trust the climate."

Nobody said anything for a second. Ankita made a small sound that wasn't quite a laugh - the sound of someone filing a sentence away to repeat later, to someone else, as evidence of something about Dhriti she'd always half-suspected.

"That's not realistic, Dhriti," Sravanthi said, and there was an edge under the words now, not unkind exactly, but the specific edge of someone who'd just been told, gently, that the thing she wanted was a smaller and more frightened version of the thing she should want. "You're describing a bet. Most people can't afford to lose that bet. I can't afford to lose that bet."

Dhriti looked at her for a moment. The restaurant noise came back into focus around them - a waiter refilling water two tables over, a burst of laughter from a birthday party near the window, the particular hum of a room full of people who had all, in their own ways, already arrived somewhere.

She didn't argue back. She didn't concede, either. She picked her water glass back up, drank from it, and let the question sit there on the table between them, unanswered on her side - not because she had nothing left to say, but because she'd already said the only part of it she was willing to say out loud tonight.

"Dhriti."

"Order the dessert menu," Dhriti said. "Meghana looks like she's about to start on our plates."

The table laughed - Meghana loudest of all, protesting through a mouthful of someone else's naan - and the moment folded itself back into the ordinary noise of the evening, gifts and gossip and someone's cousin's wedding date still not fixed. Sravanthi let it go too, on the surface. But she glanced at Dhriti twice more before the bill came, the kind of glance that was really a question still waiting to be answered, and got, each time, exactly the same thing back: nothing. Not a shrug, not a change of subject dressed up as an answer. Just Dhriti, unbothered, entirely certain of something she had no intention of explaining any further tonight.

She paid for two of the five when the bill was split, waving off Meghana's protest with a line about the promotion being Ankita's, not the table's collective problem. It was, if anyone had been counting - and nobody was, Dhriti made sure of that, the way she always did - roughly what she spent most months without remarking on it. Nine lakh a year did that. It bought a particular kind of silence too, the kind where you never had to explain why you could afford to be generous.

In the cab home, alone, phone dark in her lap, she let herself think about the question again - properly, without an audience this time.

She hadn't invented the scenario for Sravanthi's benefit. It had been sitting in her for longer than tonight, a private arithmetic she'd been running for a while now, on herself as much as on anyone else, though she hadn't told the table that part. Two years, she'd said. She believed it, too - not as a hope, but as something closer to a law she'd tested enough times in her own head to trust.

She didn't know yet what she'd do with it. That was the part she definitely wasn't going to say out loud to Sravanthi, or to anyone - that somewhere underneath the hypothetical, some part of her had already started waiting for a reason to test it against something real.

She thought, too, about the shape of the answer she'd given - how easily "richness follows capability" had come out, like something she'd rehearsed, when really she'd only ever said it out loud once before tonight, to herself, in a moment she wasn't in a hurry to examine too closely. It was one thing to believe a rule. It was another to have already started building a life around it before anyone had asked her to justify it at a dinner table.

The cab took the flyover past HITEC City, lights strung out below in long orange lines, and Dhriti watched them go by and said nothing, to no one, the whole rest of the way home.
