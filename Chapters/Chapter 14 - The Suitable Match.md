# Chapter 14 — The Suitable Match

Six months later.

The AI project had gone into production quietly, the way most real work did once the announcements and the funding conversations were behind it - Sahishnu spent his days now deep in something called deployment monitoring, a phrase Dhriti had heard enough times over dinner calls to repeat back to him without fully understanding it, and the hospital was still waiting on the first real results before anyone called it a success out loud. It sat in the background of her life now, a steady, unremarkable hum - his job going well, his voice on the phone sounding less braced than it used to, an ease settling into him by degrees that she noticed more than he seemed to.

Her own home had grown considerably less quiet in the same six months.

"Rukmini aunty's daughter is engaged now," her mother said, one Sunday evening, not looking up from the vegetables she was cutting, the words dropped into the kitchen with the particular weight of an opening move rather than an observation. "Third one from your batch this year."

"I heard."

"You heard." Saroja set the knife down, finally looking at her. "Dhriti, you're twenty-six. I'm not saying this to trouble you. I'm saying it because someone has to."

Dhriti had been expecting this conversation for weeks, in the vague way you expect a monsoon once the sky's gone the right colour of grey, and she'd decided, somewhere in that waiting, that she wasn't going to keep managing around it any longer. "There's someone, Amma."

The knife didn't move again for a long moment.

"Someone."

"His name is Sahishnu. We've been seeing each other for almost nine months now." Dhriti kept her voice level, the way she kept most difficult sentences level, though she felt her father's presence shift in the next room, the newspaper he'd been reading going still in his hands, listening now without pretending not to. "He's a developer. Works on a hospital AI project - patient records, genuinely good work, work that matters."

"A developer." Saroja said the word carefully, turning it over for the catch in it, the way she examined everything Dhriti told her these days. "At which company?"

"It's not a company like that. It's project-based, through the hospital directly."

"How much does he make?"

"Amma-"

"How much, Dhriti." Saroja's voice hadn't risen, not yet, but something in it had gone flat and immovable, a tone Dhriti recognised from a lifetime of watching her mother decide things quickly and defend them slowly, at length, without ever quite budging from the first position. "You make nine lakh. I'm asking a simple question."

"He's on six now. It was three, sixteen months ago - he lost a project through no fault of his own, took whatever work he could find to get by, and now he's back doing the thing he's actually good at, and it's already doubled in six months. That trajectory matters more than the number does today."

"Six." Saroja repeated it the way she might have repeated a diagnosis, something to be absorbed and then managed. "You earn three lakh more than him. And you're telling me this like it's meant to reassure me."

"It's meant to tell you the truth, which is more than most of the men you've sent my way have managed."

"Don't be smart with me." Saroja picked the knife back up, resumed cutting with more force than the vegetables required, and Dhriti watched her mother's jaw work through several sentences she was visibly choosing not to say yet. "I am not sending you men for sport, Dhriti. I am trying to make sure you don't spend the next twenty years explaining yourself to people who will never stop asking why you settled."

"I'm not settling. That's the part you're not hearing."

"I hear you perfectly well. I hear a girl who's found someone she likes and is now building an argument backward to justify it." Saroja didn't look up from the vegetables, though her voice had climbed now, unmistakably, filling the kitchen in a way that made Dhriti aware, distantly, of her father's chair creaking in the next room as he finally stood. "You think I don't know what a good man looks like? I'm not saying he's not good. I'm saying good and secure are not the same word, and you are old enough now to know the difference matters."

"They're not the same word. I never said they were." Dhriti kept her voice steady, refusing to match her mother's climb, though something in her chest had tightened into the familiar shape it always took during these conversations - not quite anger, something closer to the exhaustion of arguing a position she'd already proven to her own satisfaction months ago and had to keep re-proving to an audience that wasn't interested in the proof. "I'm telling you what he's capable of. You're telling me what he currently earns. Those are different facts, Amma, and you're choosing to only weigh one of them."

"The one I'm weighing is the one that pays the rent."

Prakash appeared in the kitchen doorway then, still holding the folded newspaper, his voice pitched carefully into the gap between them. "Saroja. Let her finish."

"She has finished. She's told us the boy makes six lakh and used to make three, and somehow this is supposed to reassure me."

"It's not about reassuring you today. It's about whether you're willing to actually meet him before deciding." Prakash came further into the kitchen, standing somewhere between the two of them without quite managing to insert himself fully into either woman's line of sight, the posture of a man who'd spent a long marriage learning exactly how much space he was allowed to take up in an argument that wasn't fundamentally his. "Dhriti's not a child making an impulsive choice. She's twenty-six, she has her own income, her own judgement. Maybe we hear her out properly before we decide the boy's number is the whole story."

"You always do this." Saroja turned on him now, not unkindly, but with the particular weariness of a long-married woman addressing a familiar failing. "You hear both sides and end up standing nowhere. Someone in this house has to actually decide things, Prakash."

"I'm not saying don't decide anything. I'm saying decide it after you've met him, not before."

Saroja didn't answer that directly. She turned back to the vegetables instead, the knife moving again, faster now, a clear signal that the conversation - as far as she was concerned - had reached the end of what she was willing to say tonight, even if nothing had actually been resolved.

Dhriti stood there a moment longer, in the space between her parents, and felt the particular helplessness of having said everything true and watched it change nothing. Her father caught her eye once, something apologetic in it, a silent acknowledgement that he'd done what he could and it hadn't been enough, not tonight.

"I'm not giving him up, Amma," Dhriti said quietly, to her mother's back. "I want you to hear that clearly, even if you don't agree with it yet."

Saroja didn't turn around. "We'll see," she said, which in her mother's mouth had never once meant what the words actually said - it meant *this isn't over*, and Dhriti, standing there in the kitchen with the smell of turmeric and her father's quiet, useless sympathy filling the space around her, understood exactly how much further this was going to have to go before her mother let it rest.

Her father knocked on her door a little later, after the kitchen had gone quiet and her mother's footsteps had retreated toward their own room. He didn't come in fully, just leaned against the doorframe the way he did when he had something to say that he wasn't sure he had the right to say.

"She'll come around," he said. "Not tonight. Maybe not this month. But your mother's never been someone who changes her mind quickly - she changes it slowly, and usually without admitting she's changed it at all."

"That's not exactly reassuring, Nanna."

"It's not meant to be reassuring. It's meant to be true." He allowed himself a small, tired smile. "I liked what you said about his trajectory. She heard it too, whatever she says tomorrow. Your mother doesn't miss things, Dhriti, she just decides slowly which ones she's willing to act on."

"Do you agree with her? About the money?"

He was quiet a moment, choosing his answer with the same care he seemed to choose most things. "I think a man's worth showing up on time, staying honest, and not running from difficulty. I haven't met your Sahishnu yet. If he's what you say he is, the number will sort itself out - it usually does, given enough time. I just don't know how to make your mother believe that on anyone's schedule but her own."

It wasn't a full endorsement, and she understood, even as he said it, that it was the most he was likely to give her tonight, standing in a doorway between two rooms that both wanted different things from him. She thanked him anyway, and meant it, because it was more than her mother had offered all evening.

She went to bed that night without eating much dinner, and lay awake longer than she meant to, running the conversation back over in pieces, looking for a version of it that ended differently. She didn't find one. She fell asleep instead still turning it over, the argument unfinished, the house around her settled into an uneasy, temporary quiet that she knew, even half-asleep, wasn't going to last.
