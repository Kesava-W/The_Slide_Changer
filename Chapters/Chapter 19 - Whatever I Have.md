# Chapter 19 — Whatever I Have

Manish called him on a Wednesday evening, his voice carrying a specific urgency Sahishnu hadn't heard from him since the park, months back.

"You need to know something before you hear it from anyone else," Manish said, no preamble. "Dhriti's pregnant. She told her parents two days ago. It's yours, obviously - I'm not calling to ask, I'm calling because I don't think anyone else is going to tell you and you deserve to hear it properly, not as gossip filtering down through someone's cousin."

Sahishnu sat very still on the edge of his bed, the phone pressed hard against his ear, and felt something in him go quiet in the particular way it did right before it understood something completely.

"She didn't tell me," he said, mostly to himself.

"No."

"How long has she known?"

"A while, I think. Weeks, at least." Manish's voice had gone careful, the tone of a man delivering news he suspected was going to land in more than one direction at once. "There was a suitor, Sahishnu - her mother was pushing hard, had a wedding half-arranged with some other family. Dhriti told her mother about the pregnancy two days ago, in front of her father, and apparently more or less dared her to keep the wedding on the table anyway. The suitor's family withdrew within forty-eight hours."

Sahishnu sat with that for a long moment, turning it over, something shifting into place that he realised, distantly, had been sitting half-formed in him for longer than he'd let himself examine.

He thought of the evening at his flat, months back now - the power cut, the quiet after, a closeness that had felt, at the time, like nothing more than two people finally letting go of the carefulness that had sat between them since the park. He'd wondered, once or twice since, in the space of a passing thought he'd never let finish, whether there'd been more sitting behind her eyes that evening than either of them had said out loud. He'd never asked. Some part of him, he understood now, hadn't wanted the answer badly enough to risk the question - had preferred, quietly, to let the evening stay simple rather than test whether it had been.

It hadn't been simple. He understood that now, sitting on the edge of his bed with the phone still warm against his ear, revisiting the last two months in a new and much clearer light - her happiness on quiet evenings he'd mistaken for nothing more than happiness, a certain stillness in her some days that he'd noticed and not pressed, because pressing had never once been required of him with Dhriti; she gave him what she was ready to give, on her own schedule, and he'd learned, across a year of knowing her, to trust that schedule rather than interrogate it.

She'd made a decision, alone, and carried the whole weight of it without telling him, and used it - deliberately, with her eyes open - against a mother who wouldn't listen to reason and a suitor who'd underestimated her. He didn't know yet how he felt about the shape of that, about being the one person kept outside a decision that was, in the most literal sense, also his. He suspected he'd feel several things about it, over time, not all of them comfortable.

None of that mattered more, right now, than the plain fact sitting underneath all of it.

"I need to go to her," he said.

"That's why I called first. Thought you'd want the full picture before you walked in."

"Thank you, Manish. For calling. For - " He stopped, not quite able to finish the sentence, and Manish, on the other end, seemed to understand it anyway.

"Go," Manish said. "I'll be here if either of you needs anything."

There was a pause on the line, neither of them hanging up yet, and Sahishnu found himself thinking, briefly, of the whole strange shape of the last year and Manish's place inside it - the man who'd delivered the lie that started all of it, who'd been blackmailed into doing it, who'd sat across from him in a park and told him the truth without flinching, and who was, even now, still the one making sure Sahishnu didn't have to hear the most important news of his life from a stranger. Whatever debts existed between the three of them, Sahishnu understood, standing there phone in hand, that Manish had spent the better part of a year quietly paying his back.

"We're okay, Manish," he said. "Whatever happened before. We're okay."

"I appreciate you saying that." Manish's voice had gone rough, just slightly. "Go. She needs you more than I do right now."

There was a beat of silence on the line before Manish spoke again, quieter, like he was trying the sentence out for the first time to see if it held his weight. "I think I'm going to tell my parents. About Priya. Properly - not tonight, but soon."

Sahishnu didn't ask what had changed. He thought he already knew - it was hard to watch two people choose each other in front of everyone who might disapprove and go on quietly hiding your own choice the next morning. "Good," he said simply. "Tell her I said so, whenever you do."

Manish laughed, short and startled, like the sound had gotten out before he'd decided to allow it. "I will."

---

He didn't call ahead. He changed his shirt without really registering doing it, grabbed his wallet and keys, and was in an auto within ten minutes, giving the driver Dhriti's address in a voice that came out steadier than he felt, the whole ride there spent staring out at a city going past in streaks of ordinary evening light, none of it registering.

The house, when the auto pulled up, was lit only in a couple of windows, the rest of it dark in a way that felt less like an empty house and more like a house that had simply stopped having the energy for anything beyond the essentials the last two days. He paid the driver without really registering the amount, stood at the gate a moment, and felt, for the first time since Manish's call, something close to nervousness arrive - not about what he was going to say, that part had arranged itself in his head somewhere on the ride over without him consciously composing it, but about whether he'd be let past the gate at all.

There was no version of this, arriving at her gate, where he'd stopped to weigh the cost first. That specifically struck him, standing there for a moment before he rang the bell - how completely absent the old calculation was, the one that had sat behind every decision he'd made about Dhriti since the day Manish first brought her name to him. Three lakh a year. Nine lakh a year. What people would say, two years from now, at some family function. None of it occurred to him even once on the ride over. It simply wasn't part of the arithmetic anymore, and he understood, standing at her gate with his thumb on the bell, that it hadn't been part of the arithmetic for a while now - only he hadn't noticed the moment it had stopped mattering.

Prakash opened the door himself.

He looked older than Sahishnu remembered from the one photo Dhriti had once shown him, tired in a way that went past a single bad week, and something in his face, seeing Sahishnu standing there, shifted - not surprise exactly, more like a man recognising that the conversation he'd been dreading had finally, predictably, arrived.

"Sahishnu." Prakash said his name carefully, testing it.

"Sir." Sahishnu stood there, not stepping forward uninvited, his hands loose at his sides, and found, to his own quiet surprise, that his voice held steady straight through what came next. "I know what's happened. I know Dhriti hasn't - I know I'm hearing this later than I should have, and that's a conversation between the two of us, not one I need to have with you tonight. What I came to say is simpler than that."

Prakash didn't say anything, just watched him, waiting.

"I love your daughter. I've loved her for longer than either of us was probably honest about, even to ourselves. Whatever's happened, whatever she decided and however she decided it - I'm not walking away from this, from her, or from the child. I'll marry her, if she'll still have me, as soon as it can be arranged. I don't have much yet. I have a job that's paying properly now, for the first time in years, and it's only going to get better from here - I believe that, and I'll prove it, not just say it. But whatever I have, all of it, is hers. And the child's. That's not a promise I'm making lightly, sir. I mean every word of it."

Prakash stood in the doorway a long moment, silent, something working behind his eyes that Sahishnu couldn't fully read - grief, still, from two days ago, mixed now with something that looked, unmistakably, like relief, the particular relief of a father who'd spent two days afraid of exactly this conversation and was only now realising it wasn't going to go the way he'd feared.

"Come in," Prakash said finally, stepping back from the door. "She's upstairs. I think she needs to hear this from you directly, not secondhand from me."

Sahishnu stepped inside, into a house he'd never once been welcomed into before tonight, and felt, walking through the doorway, the specific weight of every hesitation he'd carried in the park months ago finally, completely, falling away behind him.
